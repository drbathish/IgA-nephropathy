# Recent (2022–present) advances in the diagnosis, pathogenesis, and treatment of IgA nephropathy (IgAN) and IgA vasculitis (IgAV)

## Scope

This review addresses: what are the recent (January 2022–present) advances in the diagnosis, pathogenesis, and treatment of IgA nephropathy (IgAN) and IgA vasculitis (IgAV, including IgA vasculitis nephritis [IgAVN]), across both clinical and basic/mechanistic research?

**Population/model:** Human patients — pediatric and adult — with biopsy-confirmed or clinically diagnosed IgAN and/or IgAV/IgAVN, plus human kidney tissue/biopsy specimens, human cell lines or primary cells, and animal models explicitly used to model IgAN or IgAV pathogenesis (e.g., Gd-IgA1 immune-complex models, gddY mice, humanized IGHA1 mice, HSP mouse models).

**Intervention/exposure/mechanism:** Pharmacologic and biologic therapies specific to IgAN or IgAV (SGLT2 inhibitors, endothelin receptor antagonists, targeted-release budesonide, complement pathway inhibitors, BAFF/APRIL inhibitors, B-cell-depleting agents, RAAS blockade, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, immunosuppressants); diagnostic/prognostic biomarkers (Gd-IgA1, anti-glycan autoantibodies, multi-omics markers); and pathogenic mechanisms (mucosal immunity/gut–kidney axis, complement activation, genetic risk, immune-complex/mesangial deposition).

**Date range:** January 2022–present. **Language:** English only.

**Topic-specific constraints applied throughout this report, per research-question.md:** (1) IgAN and IgAV findings are reported under separate headings even where a single study or shared mechanism spans both diseases; (2) any extrapolation of IgAN-derived therapies (e.g., TRF-budesonide, complement inhibitors, BAFF/APRIL inhibitors, sparsentan) to IgAV is explicitly labeled indirect evidence, never presented as IgAV-specific confirmation.

## Findings

### IgA Nephropathy (IgAN)

#### Pathogenesis and mechanism

As background, IgAN is understood to arise from a multi-hit process involving galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) production, anti-glycan autoantibody formation, immune-complex deposition, and mesangial injury, with complement activation (particularly the lectin and alternative pathways) increasingly recognized as a key amplifying step [Cheung et al., 2024, background; Barratt et al., 2023, background]. Within the included primary evidence, a case-control immunological study found that circulating Gd-IgA1-producing B cells in IgAN patients preferentially carry lambda light chains and mucosal (gut/respiratory-tract) homing receptors, supporting a mucosal origin for Gd-IgA1 production, though the authors themselves note this was a single-center European cohort with unconfirmed generalizability to other populations [Zachova et al., 2022]. A mixed human case-control and murine mechanistic study found that gut dysbiosis (Escherichia-Shigella enrichment) correlated with Gd-IgA1 levels and TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway activation in IgAN patients, and that fecal microbiota transplantation from IgAN patients reproduced an IgAN-like phenotype in recipient mice — the murine causal component strengthens inference beyond the human cross-sectional/case-control data alone, but the human arm remains correlational [Zhu et al., 2024]. As background, a narrative review notes that no prospective cohort has yet specifically characterized the gut microbiome in pediatric primary IgAN, extrapolating adult-cohort mechanistic data to pediatric populations via a "developmental entanglement" framework and explicitly flagging this extrapolation — and its own further extension to pediatric IgA vasculitis nephritis — as unvalidated and indirect, not confirmed evidence [Zhou et al., 2026, background]. A novel humanized IGHA1 transgenic mouse model (crossed with a complement factor H mutant) reproduced IgAN-like glomerular IgA deposition, hematuria, and albuminuria following mucosal stimulation with Lactobacillus casei cell wall extract, providing a new preclinical tool for mucosal-trigger hypotheses but no human efficacy/safety data [Li et al., 2024/2025]. A PRISMA-guided tier-1 systematic review of 34 studies (10,082 patients) found that complement proteins from the alternative and lectin pathways were specifically associated with the crescentic (C1-2) Oxford MEST-C class, supporting the biological rationale for complement-pathway-targeted therapies in more histologically active disease — though this review was heavily weighted toward Asian-population studies (8,128 of 10,082 patients), a generalizability caveat [Ștefan et al., 2023].

A subsequent preclinical mouse study directly tested whether Gd-IgA1 elevation alone is sufficient to drive glomerular deposition, using a humanized IGHA1 knock-in mouse with B-cell-specific c1galt1 deletion: this genetic manipulation markedly elevated circulating Gd-IgA1, both at baseline and after inflammatory mucosal stimulation, and impaired B-cell development and IgA production — but glomerular IgA deposition remained limited and was NOT substantially enhanced by inflammatory stimulation despite the markedly elevated Gd-IgA1. In a complementary passive-transfer experiment, mucosa (ileocecal)-derived IgA1 from IgAN patients caused significantly stronger mesangial deposition than serum- or myeloma-derived IgA1, despite similar or lower Gd-IgA1 content; the authors interpret this as evidence that Gd-IgA1 is "a correlate of mucosal immune activation rather than a direct pathogenic driver," with tissue origin/immune context as the more likely determinant of deposition and pathogenicity [Wu et al., 2026]. This finding nuances — it does not overturn — the Gd-IgA1-centered multi-hit pathogenic model described as background above, and sits in tension with the mucosal-origin case-control evidence [Zachova et al., 2022] and gut-dysbiosis evidence [Zhu et al., 2024] in this table, as well as with the biological rationale underlying several complement- and BAFF/APRIL-targeted IgAN therapies discussed under Treatment below; this tension is flagged explicitly, not resolved, in Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty. This is preclinical mouse-model evidence only, exact group sample sizes were not extractable from the available (paywalled) full text, and its interpretive conclusion is the study authors' own, not an independently confirmed finding of this review. As background, a subsequent conceptual/perspective re-analysis proposes that only a defined subset of circulating Gd-IgA1 — distinguished by mucosal origin, molecular properties favoring selective mesangial deposition, and the capacity to form immune complexes and activate complement — may be the truly pathogenic fraction, offering one possible interpretive frame for reconciling this Gd-IgA1-level-versus-deposition tension; this re-analysis of previously published trial data generates no new primary data of its own and is not itself evidence resolving the tension described above [Nihei & Suzuki, 2026, background].

A case-control immunophenotyping study of 66 IgAN patients and 30 healthy controls, with an independent validation cohort of 179 IgAN patients, 140 minimal-change-disease patients, and 91 healthy controls, found significantly higher circulating total IgA, Gd-IgA1, and APRIL in IgAN patients, together with significantly higher class-switched memory and double-negative B cells and significantly lower mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells and γδ T cells; the MAIT-cell reduction was independently validated in the larger cohort and was IgAN-specific rather than seen in the minimal-change-disease comparator [Gentile et al., 2024]. The study authors themselves interpret this alongside an apparent contrast with an RCT — cited by the authors but not itself an included study in this evidence table, and therefore not independently verified here — reporting no efficacy of rituximab (a B-cell-depleting agent) in IgAN, speculating that peripheral B-cell depletion doses may not adequately deplete gut-mucosal B cells; this is the study authors' own interpretive hypothesis for why a demonstrated B-cell-subset abnormality might not translate into B-cell-depletion therapeutic benefit, not a confirmed finding of this review, since the underlying rituximab RCT itself is outside this evidence table (see Evidence Gaps). A separate, directly-verified rituximab RCT for IgAN (RITA) is now included in this evidence table and discussed under Treatment below [Ouyang et al., 2026, RITA]; it is a distinct trial from the one Gentile et al. cite secondhand here (which predates RITA's 2026 publication), though its own null result is directionally consistent with that externally-cited RCT's reported lack of benefit — a consistent pattern across two different trials, not confirmation that they are the same study.

Two further preclinical mechanistic studies extend the target-discovery evidence base, both at an early, hypothesis-generating stage. A mixed human-PBMC, murine, and in-vitro study found IL-6R, gp130, and STAT3 mRNA significantly upregulated in PBMCs from IgAN patients versus controls, and that Dioscin — the active compound of a traditional Chinese medicine formula — suppressed IL-6-induced Gd-IgA1/IgA secretion and JAK2/STAT3 pathway activation in an IgA1-secreting B-lymphocyte cell line and reduced mesangial IgA deposition in a mouse model of IgAN; the authors themselves state that Dioscin is currently approved only for coronary heart disease (not IgAN), that mice cannot produce Gd-IgA1 so no direct in vivo evidence of Gd-IgA1 inhibition was obtained, and that no pathway-inhibition or gene-knockout experiments were performed to directly validate the proposed target [Lin et al., 2025]. A separate mixed mouse-model, cell-line, and human-bioinformatics study reported that SOCS1 expression was suppressed in renal tissue and PBMCs of IgAN patients relative to controls (consistent with an independent public dataset), correlating positively with C1GALT1 and negatively with TLR9/MyD88, and that manipulating SOCS1 in vitro and in vivo altered Gd-IgA1 secretion and glomerular IgA/IgG/C3 deposition, proposing a SOCS1-TLR9-MyD88-C1GALT1 regulatory axis [Huang et al., 2025]. Exact sample sizes and statistical values for this study were not extractable from the available abstract-only text; both studies are preclinical/mechanistic only, with no clinical efficacy or human interventional data.

A further preclinical, mechanistic/therapeutic-target study tested sarsasapogenin (SAR), a steroidal sapogenin natural compound, in a randomized, blinded rat model of experimental IgAN induced via sequential BSA (intragastric)/CCl4 (subcutaneous)/LPS (intravenous) administration (final analysis n=7/group across four groups — control, SAR-only, IgAN+vehicle, IgAN+SAR — total n=28; single-center, Turkey). Relative to untreated IgAN animals, SAR significantly attenuated mesangial IgA deposition, reduced proteinuria at weeks 10 and 12, and reduced serum urea/creatinine, IL-6, TGF-β1/SMAD3/PDGF, and glomerular fibronectin/α-SMA, though renal parameters were not fully normalized to healthy-control levels; SAR also reduced the prevalence of Oxford MEST-C M1 and E1 lesions specifically (from 100% in untreated IgAN to 28.6% with SAR), while reductions in S1, T1, and C1 prevalence were smaller and, per the study authors' own discussion, non-significant; and SAR partially restored podocyte nephrin/podocin expression and partially preserved foot-process architecture on electron microscopy, again without full normalization to healthy-group levels [Kumaş et al., 2026]. This study used computer-generated randomization and blinding of histopathological/immunohistochemical assessment (including two independently blinded pathologists for MEST-C scoring) — a methodological strength relative to several other tier-6 preclinical rows in this evidence base, which do not report blinding — and was funded only academically (no industry sponsorship, no conflicts declared). It nonetheless remains a small (n=7/group), single-center, 12-week preclinical rat study: the authors themselves state this duration captures only the early-to-moderate disease stage and cannot address long-term efficacy or late-stage toxicity, that a definitive causal mechanism for the observed TGF-β1/SMAD3 and IL-6 pathway modulation remains unestablished absent knockout or receptor-blocking experiments, and that species differences warrant caution in extrapolating the MEST-C-scored findings to humans; most quantitative effect sizes were also presented only graphically in the paper's figures rather than as printed values in the running text. This finding is preclinical mouse/rat-model evidence only, with no clinical efficacy or safety data in humans, and complements — without contradicting — the other natural-compound/TCM-derived preclinical mechanistic findings discussed above [Lin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025] and in the IgAV section below [Lang et al., 2026], each targeting overlapping inflammatory/TGF-β-SMAD pathways; it additionally contributes podocyte-ultrastructure (electron-microscopy) evidence not present in those other rows.

As further background, complement-targeted therapeutics across glomerular diseases more broadly — using IgAN as an illustrative worked example — have been reviewed narratively elsewhere [Wade et al., 2026, background]. Directly within the included primary evidence, a confocal-microscopy immunopathology study of 17 biopsy-proven IgAN kidney biopsies (plus 3 additional biopsies used solely for confirmatory immunoblotting) found that complement C3 colocalized with glomerular IgA significantly more than with IgG (p<0.0001), and that this C3-IgA colocalization was significantly greater in biopsies with active histologic lesions — mesangial hypercellularity M1 (p=0.047), endocapillary hypercellularity E1 (p=0.018), and cellular/fibrocellular crescents C1+2 (p=0.019) — with a significant positive correlation between the summed Oxford M+E+C score and C3-IgA colocalization; immunoblotting confirmed intact (not cleaved) complement C3 within extracted immune-complex deposits [Novak et al., 2025 — IgAN]. This is a single-center (Cedars-Sinai), small (n=17), retrospective, cross-sectional study that cannot itself establish causality, though the authors cite supporting passive-transfer mouse data; it converges with and mechanistically extends the MEST-C/complement-activation association already described above [Ștefan et al., 2023]. **This study carries a corrigendum caveat handled identically to the Maisons et al. (2026) precedent described elsewhere in this report: a published corrigendum (Published Erratum) exists for this paper, and its existence is confirmed, but its substantive content has been confirmed inaccessible through the verification paths attempted (PubMed metadata and the Crossref API confirm only the correction's existence/bibliographic linkage, not what was corrected); the Pearson-correlation and p-values reported above have not been checked against the corrigendum, and this should be treated as a caveat on these specific figures, not a reason to discount the study outright** [Novak et al., 2025 — IgAN].

A further three-phase mechanistic pilot study extends the complement-pathway mechanistic evidence base with a distinct, exosome-mediated signaling link. In a clinical cohort (70 children with IgAN, 43 with other glomerulopathies, 30 healthy controls), urinary exosomal C3a normalized to creatinine (uE-C3a/Cr) was elevated in IgAN and correlated with log 24-hour urine protein (ρ=0.480, p<0.001) and the Oxford C (crescent) score (ρ=0.565, p<0.001); the AUC for distinguishing IgAN from other glomerulopathies was only 0.637, and the authors explicitly state this is NOT presented as a disease-specific diagnostic marker. uE-C3a/Cr decreased with remission and was higher in non-remission versus remission patients on immunosuppressive therapy at 12 weeks (p<0.001). In a complementary IgAN-model rat experiment, renal exosomal C3a, the NF-κB p-p65/t-p65 ratio, and MCP-1/IL-6 were all elevated; in vitro, patient urinary exosomes promoted rat mesangial-cell proliferation and NF-κB activation/cytokine release, an effect partially reversed by a C3aR antagonist. The authors' own conclusion is that uE-C3a/Cr is a candidate non-invasive disease-activity/treatment-response monitoring marker (not a diagnostic one), and that C3aR is a candidate therapeutic target [Liu et al., 2026 (exosomal C3a/C3aR→NF-κB)]. This is explicitly characterized by the authors as a small pilot study requiring larger-cohort validation, with a modest, non-diagnostic AUC; the pediatric-only clinical cohort limits generalizability to adult IgAN; full text was not obtained (no PMCID on record), so extraction is abstract/metadata-level only, with author-stated limitations beyond "small pilot study" and funding/conflict-of-interest disclosures not extractable from the available text. This mechanistic finding adds a specific exosome-mediated C3a/C3aR-to-mesangial-NF-κB signaling link to the complement-pathway evidence discussed above [Ștefan et al., 2023; Novak et al., 2025 — IgAN] and complements, without being directly tested against, the therapeutic complement-inhibitor trial evidence discussed under Treatment below [rows testing iptacopan, ravulizumab, cemdisiran].

A further preclinical/mechanistic study combining human PBMC data with a Trim21-knockout mouse model found that TRIM21 expression was significantly reduced in PBMCs from IgAN patients and inversely correlated with serum IgA levels, and that Trim21-knockout mice subjected to oral mucosal immunization developed a more severe phenotype — increased urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio and serum creatinine, impaired intestinal barrier integrity, and expanded IgA+ B cells in gut-associated lymphoid tissue — than wild-type controls; the authors propose a TRIM21-IκBα-NF-κB-AID regulatory axis that normally restrains intestinal IgA class-switching, with TRIM21 loss relieving this restraint and enhancing IgA class switching [Chen et al., 2026 (TRIM21/AID class-switching)]. This is a preclinical/mechanistic study only, with no clinical efficacy or intervention data in humans; full text was unavailable, so extraction was limited to the abstract, and exact sample sizes and specific correlation coefficients/p-values beyond directional significance were not extractable from the available text. It extends the gut-kidney-axis/mucosal-immunity mechanism sub-topic described above [Zachova et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2024] with a distinct, specific molecular regulator of the IgA class-switching step, and the "potential therapeutic target" framing proposed by the authors is hypothesis-generating only, not yet validated with any targeted intervention.

A further exploratory human multi-omics study profiled renal tissue (7 IgAN patients vs. 10 controls) and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs; 6 IgAN patients vs. 25 controls, unpaired with the renal-tissue cohort) in China, identifying 92 differentially abundant secretory proteins in IgAN renal tissue versus controls and 36 differentially-modified secretory proteins (120 lysine-crotonylation sites) in IgAN PBMCs versus controls. Protein-protein-interaction network analysis prioritized 10 exploratory hub candidates (including fibrinogen chains, complement C3, haptoglobin, and transferrin) and computationally predicted four candidate upstream transcription factors (STAT1, PML, STAT5B, MYH11); a database-derived compound-enrichment analysis (DSigDB/Enrichr) additionally flagged thapsigargin as a hypothesis-generating compound associated with the secretory-protein network [Chen et al., 2026 (renal-tissue/PBMC multi-omics)]. The study authors themselves state explicitly that "because the datasets were unpaired and the proteomic and crotonylomic samples were pooled within groups, the results do not establish participant-level cross-tissue relationships, causal regulation, validated biomarkers, or therapeutic efficacy," and frame the work as providing only "exploratory molecular insights" despite the limited sample size. This is a very small (as few as 6–10 per arm), single-center, single-country, cross-sectional study with no individual-level pairing between the renal-tissue and PBMC cohorts and no clinical or functional validation of the computationally predicted hub proteins or transcription factors; it should be read as hypothesis-generating only, not as an established IgAN biomarker or mechanism, and adds a distinct secretory-protein/lysine-crotonylation angle to the mechanistic evidence base alongside the other tier-6 studies discussed above.

As further background, IgAN pathogenesis has also been framed through a refined "four-hit" model with complement amplification, alongside risk-stratified management built around RAAS blockade/SGLT2-inhibition with escalation to glucocorticoids or targeted therapies in higher-risk patients [Zhou et al., 2026 (*Clinical Nephrology*), background — labeled "Systematic Review" by PubMed's own article-type field, but evidence-reviewer determined its abstract describes no PRISMA-style search strategy, databases, or quantitative synthesis, so it is treated here as a narrative review for background/context only, not as the evidentiary basis for any specific quantitative claim; distinct from the unrelated "Zhou et al., 2026" gut-microbiome background source discussed elsewhere in this report]. Two further newly identified preclinical/mechanistic studies add distinct candidate pathogenic pathways to this evidence base. A mixed human serum/renal-tissue, mouse-model, and in vitro study found neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) significantly elevated in IgAN patients' serum/renal tissue and in a mouse IgAN model; NETs induced glomerular endothelial injury and glycocalyx shedding in vitro via cGAS-STING pathway activation, an effect substantially reversed by DNase I-mediated NET degradation, pharmacologic cGAS/STING inhibitors, or siRNA knockdown of cGAS/STING [Li et al., 2026 (NETs/cGAS-STING)]. This is purely preclinical/mechanistic evidence with no human interventional data; exact human sample sizes, statistical effect sizes, and author-stated limitations were not extractable from the available abstract-only text, and it adds an innate-immunity/neutrophil axis mechanistically distinct from, and complementary to, the complement- and BAFF/APRIL-pathway mechanisms already represented in this evidence base. Separately, a human kidney-biopsy immunofluorescence study (20 IgAN biopsies vs. 7 histologically normal controls, Portugal/Norway) found peroxidasin (PXDN) expression reduced in proximal and distal tubules but significantly increased in fibrotic interstitium versus both controls and non-fibrotic IgAN regions (p<0.01 to p<0.001), strongly correlating with collagen III deposition in sclerotic glomeruli (r=0.82, p=0.034) and fibrotic interstitium (r=0.93, p=0.006), with higher glomerular PXDN independently associated with eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73m² (p<0.0001) and negatively correlated with eGFR (r=−0.55, p=0.012) [Silva et al., 2026]. The study authors themselves explicitly characterize this as a "modest" single-center cohort (n=20) with a cross-sectional design precluding causal inference, note that FFPE-only material prevented functional/mRNA-level validation, and describe the proposed PXDN-collagen-III mechanistic link as "speculative" pending functional validation; they also report a methodological caution that nephrectomy-derived "normal" control tissue showed lower PXDN intensity than biopsy-derived normal tissue (attributed to longer ischemic intervals before fixation), which they note strengthens confidence in their chosen biopsy-derived comparator. This identifies PXDN as a novel candidate biomarker/therapeutic target for interstitial fibrosis in IgAN, adding a distinct angle to the mechanistic evidence base above, with no clinical interventional data.

A further newly identified preclinical mechanistic/therapeutic-development study tested extracellular vesicles (EVs) isolated from polarized peritoneal M2 macrophages in a murine IgAN model and an in vitro LPS-injured podocyte model. Three weeks of EV treatment improved survival and body weight, reduced proteinuria, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, serum creatinine, and BUN, and attenuated mesangial expansion, podocyte effacement, fibrosis, and mesangial IgA deposition; proteomic profiling showed a shift toward a healthier renal protein signature, with enrichment of redox-balance pathways and downregulation of fibrosis/innate-immunity/inflammation pathways; EVs also reduced renal mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, increased SOD2 expression, restored PI3K/AKT phosphorylation in LPS-injured podocytes, decreased the renal M1/M2 macrophage ratio, increased regulatory T-cell accumulation, and reduced inflammatory cytokine expression [Zhang et al., 2026, M2 macrophage EV nanotherapeutic]. This is entirely preclinical (murine and in vitro) evidence with no human data; specific numeric effect sizes/p-values, the exact murine strain and IgAN-induction method, per-group sample sizes, and the authors' own stated limitations were not extractable from the available abstract-only text (no PMCID on record, full text not obtained). This finding identifies a novel candidate nanotherapeutic mechanism — immune-oxidative microenvironment reprogramming via M2-macrophage-derived EVs — mechanistically distinct from the named pharmacologic/biologic therapy classes and from the complement- and BAFF/APRIL-pathway agents already well-represented in this evidence base's Treatment subsection below.

As further background, an "oral-kidney axis" framework has been proposed linking collagen-binding cnm-positive *Streptococcus mutans* (an oral bacterium) to IgAN pathogenesis, extending the multi-hit hypothesis and mucosal-immunity framework [Misaki et al., 2026, background]. Directly within the included primary evidence, a further newly identified human mechanistic study examined tonsillar tissue, renal tissue, peripheral blood, and an in vitro co-culture system to test a CXCR3-IP-10 axis as a candidate mechanistic link between tonsillar focal infection and Gd-IgA1 production. CXCR3+ cells accumulated more densely in interfollicular areas of IgAN tonsils than non-IgAN tonsils; CXCR3+ cells and IP-10 were detected in the renal tubulointerstitium, with multiplex immunofluorescence showing CXCR3/CD3 co-localization predominantly there; tonsillar mononuclear cells from IgAN patients showed a significantly higher chemotactic response to IP-10 than comparators; and in co-culture experiments, CXCR3+ T cells enhanced Gd-IgA1 production by tonsillar B cells. Tonsillar CXCR3+CD3+ cell proportions were higher in patients who achieved clinical remission after tonsillectomy, and circulating CXCR3+CD3+ cells decreased after tonsillectomy in IgAN patients but not in non-IgAN patients [Wakisaka et al., 2026]. This is a fundamentally correlative/mechanistic study: it supports but does not establish causality for the CXCR3-IP-10 axis driving Gd-IgA1 production and renal T-cell recruitment, and the remission-after-tonsillectomy and pre/post-tonsillectomy comparisons are observational (not randomized), so selection bias in which patients underwent tonsillectomy cannot be excluded from the abstract alone. The exact sample size is not stated in the available abstract, and most statistical comparisons are reported only in qualitative "significantly higher/greater" language rather than as specific p-values; full text was not obtained (no PMCID on record), so the authors' own stated limitations and funding/conflict-of-interest disclosures are likewise not extractable. This adds a specific candidate molecular mechanism supporting the tonsillar-focal-disease/mucosal-immunity hypothesis already represented in this evidence base [Zachova et al., 2022] and in the background literature just described [Misaki et al., 2026, background] — complementary to, not in conflict with, that existing literature.

#### Diagnostics and biomarkers

As background, hematuria has been proposed as a key risk factor for CKD progression across glomerular diseases more broadly, including heme/hemoglobin-cytotoxicity mechanisms and macroscopic-hematuria-precipitated AKI in IgAN [Gutiérrez et al., 2026, background]. Directly within the included primary evidence, a newly identified PRISMA-2020-compliant, PROSPERO-registered (CRD420261282641) tier-1 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 8 independent cohorts (9 reports, n=4,776 total, 423 kidney events in the primary analysis) and found that persistent/longitudinal microscopic hematuria was independently associated with kidney disease progression in IgAN: pooled HR 2.64 (95% CI 2.00–3.47, p<0.0001, I²=0%; Hartung-Knapp-adjusted HR 2.64, 95% CI 2.25–3.09), robust to leave-one-out and cohort-substitution sensitivity analyses [Berti et al., 2026]. An exploratory pooled eGFR-slope analysis (4 studies) found a mean difference of −2.58 mL/min/1.73m²/year under conventional random-effects modeling, but the authors themselves explicitly flag this as "hypothesis-generating rather than definitive" since the more conservative Hartung-Knapp-adjusted confidence interval crossed the null; they likewise caution that residual confounding cannot be excluded across the pooled cohorts' varying adjustment strategies, so the pooled estimates should be read as evidence of an independent prognostic association rather than proof of a causal effect. This GRADE-for-prognostic-factors, QUIPS-risk-of-bias-assessed systematic review directly complements the existing MEST-C/complement systematic review discussed above [Ștefan et al., 2023] and is one of the highest-quality new additions to this evidence base. **This study's final author surname is given as "Martitati" by PubMed and "Maritati" by Crossref — a bibliographic discrepancy carried forward without adjudication per the anti-fabrication rule against silently correcting a verified record, and not a substantive issue affecting the reported findings.**

A separate, newly identified large (n=1,283) single-center retrospective pediatric IgAN cohort (Jinling Hospital, China, 2000–2020, median follow-up 122 months) examined red blood cell casts (RBCC) on kidney biopsy — present in 13.5% of patients — as a prognostic marker, and found that RBCC positivity, despite being associated with a more active clinical presentation (e.g., gross hematuria), was independently associated with a significantly *lower* risk of the composite kidney endpoint (adjusted HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.32–0.85), with the protective association strongest in patients with preserved kidney function (eGFR≥60: HR 0.14) and weaker or non-significant in those with reduced eGFR or on immunosuppressive therapy [Wu et al., 2026, RBC casts]. **This finding should be read alongside, not as contradicting, the Berti et al. 2026 persistent-hematuria finding immediately above: the two studies measure different constructs — RBC casts are a point-in-time histologic marker of active glomerular bleeding captured at a single biopsy, whereas Berti et al.'s exposure is longitudinal, persistent urinary hematuria tracked over time via serial urinalysis. The evidence table explicitly flags this as a directional tension to be surfaced, not smoothed over, rather than a resolved contradiction (see Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty below).** This is an abstract-level-only extraction (full text paywalled), a single-center retrospective design limiting generalizability, and the weaker protective association in immunosuppressed patients raises the possibility of confounding by indication (sicker patients preferentially treated) that is not resolved in the available text.

A separate small (n=3) case series from India, included via the tier-7 "clinically significant diagnostic pitfall" carve-out, describes IgAN presenting with chronic anterior ocular inflammation (2 recurrent episcleritis, 1 scleritis) that significantly preceded the renal diagnosis in all 3 cases; baseline urinalysis — prompted by the ocular symptoms — revealed dysmorphic microscopic hematuria and marked proteinuria (2.0–4.0 g/day), and renal biopsy confirmed IgAN in all three, including one with crescent formation [Dhanshetty & Rapur, 2026]. The authors' explicit clinical message is to incorporate urine-dipstick screening into the routine workup of inflammatory eye disease to catch IgAN earlier; this is very low-evidentiary-weight evidence (n=3, no control group, no statistical inference possible, single institution) and should not be generalized beyond illustrating this diagnostic pattern, but it was judged a comparatively higher-confidence fit for the tier-7 carve-out than several of this evidence base's other contested tier-7 inclusions, since it concerns diagnostic recognition of IgAN itself rather than managing or attributing a complication of already-diagnosed disease.

A further newly identified single-center retrospective cohort (n=153 biopsy-proven IgAN, South Korea) examined the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI, an immunonutritional-status composite marker) stratified into four groups, finding that the lowest-PNI group (<40) had a 3.49-fold higher UPCR at biopsy (adjusted mean ratio 3.49, p<0.001) and was independently associated with segmental sclerosis/S1 (OR 5.09, p=0.029) and crescents/C1-2 (OR 3.81, p=0.031), while the highest-PNI group (≥55) showed lower UPCR, less advanced tubular atrophy/fibrosis, and lower Haas stages [Kim et al., 2026]. The composite kidney-outcome association was attenuated after UPCR adjustment in the total cohort, but the lowest-PNI group retained a very large point estimate (HR 17.99, p=0.006) among the subgroup with non-nephrotic-range proteinuria specifically; the evidence table flags that this striking estimate's subgroup size, event count, and confidence interval are not extractable from the available abstract, so its precision cannot be independently assessed and it should not be treated as a stand-alone headline figure. This is a single-center, single-ethnicity cohort with generalizability to other populations unestablished, and abstract-level extraction only.

A further newly identified single-center retrospective cross-sectional cohort (n=71 adults with biopsy-proven primary IgAN, Japan; median systemic immune-inflammation index [SII] 508.87) examined SII, a blood-count-derived composite inflammatory biomarker, against histological lesion burden assessed at the same biopsy. In the fully adjusted (age, sex, eGFR, proteinuria) model, acute histological lesions showed a positive but statistically non-significant association with SII (β=10.615, 95% CI −0.629 to 21.860, p=0.064); chronic lesions showed no association (p=0.871). In a sensitivity analysis adjusting only for age and eGFR, the acute-lesion association became statistically significant (p=0.043). An exploratory ROC analysis for identifying severe active lesions (>10% acute lesions, only n=10 positive cases) yielded an AUC of 0.725 (95% CI 0.554–0.848), with an exploratory cutoff of 618.8 giving 70.0% sensitivity and 67.2% specificity [Miyazaki et al., 2026]. **This is an explicitly fragile, inconsistently significant finding that should not be presented as a robust, confirmed association**: statistical significance for the primary acute-lesion association depends on which covariates are adjusted for — non-significant (p=0.064) in the fully adjusted model, significant (p=0.043) only in the reduced sensitivity-analysis model — and the ROC/cutoff analysis rests on just 10 positive cases, raising a precision/overfitting concern not addressed in the available text. The study authors themselves conclude only that higher SII "may be associated with acute glomerular lesions," explicitly stating its "clinical utility requires validation in larger cohorts." This is a small, single-center study with abstract-level extraction only (no PMCID on record, full text not obtained).

Finally, a newly identified multicenter retrospective diagnostic-accuracy study (n=592 biopsy-confirmed pediatric patients across 3 Chinese centers) evaluated an automated ultrasound "habitat radiomics" tool (deep-learning kidney segmentation plus radiomics-feature classification) for distinguishing IgAN from IgA vasculitis nephritis (HSPN). **This is a single joint two-class differentiation model, not separately-reported disease-specific findings: the paper does not report IgAN-only or HSPN-only accuracy metrics, so the same shared result is reported here for the IgAN subgroup (n=254 of 592) and, separately per this report's disease-separation convention, for the HSPN/IgAV subgroup under IgA Vasculitis Diagnostics below — stated explicitly here, consistent with the evidence table's own instruction not to attribute a pooled, non-separable result to one disease subgroup as if it were independently derived.** The combined radiomics-plus-clinical model achieved an AUC of 0.846 internally and 0.805 externally for distinguishing the two diseases, versus 0.717/0.733 for a clinical-variables-only model, with the radiomics-only model achieving AUC 0.806 internally and 0.825 externally [Gong et al., 2026]. This is abstract-level-only extraction (ahead-of-print, no PMCID), the external-validation cohort (n=139) is modest, and an AUC in the 0.80–0.85 range still indicates a clinically meaningful minority of cases would be misclassified between two diseases with different systemic-disease implications despite similar renal histology.

A single-center deep-learning study trained and validated a neural network for automated Oxford MEST-C scoring on IgAN biopsies (total n=196 across training/test/application cohorts) and found that network-derived S and T scores were prognostic for a composite kidney-survival endpoint (HR 9.67 and 7.67 respectively); this is a small, single-center cohort pending external validation [Jaugey et al., 2023]. A subsequent, considerably larger multicenter study extended this digital-pathology diagnostic trend: a retrospective analysis of the European VALIGA cohort (n=555 adults with biopsy-proven IgAN, centralized MEST-C histopathology, median follow-up 7.3 years) derived deep-learning "pathomics" quantitative morphological biomarkers from digitized biopsy slides and compared their prognostic performance against traditional MEST-C scoring and the existing International IgAN Prediction Tool (IIgAN-PT) for a composite kidney-failure/eGFR-decline outcome (148/555 events). Pathomics alone modestly outperformed MEST-C alone (ΔC-statistic +0.04), but was marginally worse than MEST-C once clinical predictors were added, and adding pathomics on top of the existing IIgAN-PT gave only a minor further improvement (ΔC-statistic +0.024); the authors conclude pathomics is "noninferior" to MEST-C — an objective, reproducible alternative rather than a large incremental predictive gain [Hölscher et al., 2026]. This was an internally (bootstrap-)validated, retrospective, predominantly White/European cohort (97% White) with no external validation in an independent cohort, and the authors themselves caution that findings are primarily generalizable to European/White populations; two authors disclosed consulting/research-funding relationships with multiple pharmaceutical/AI companies, stated by the authors as unrelated to this specific work. Despite its considerably larger, multicenter, outcome-validated design relative to the single-center Jaugey et al. study above, this remains fundamentally a proof-of-concept model-comparison study, not a clinical-outcomes intervention trial. A single-center retrospective cross-sectional study (n=256) found that hyperhomocysteinemia (32% of patients) was associated with worse renal function, higher inflammatory/tubular-injury markers, and more severe tubulointerstitial fibrosis, with an exploratory homocysteine cutoff of 12.50 μmol/L (AUC 0.756); however, the authors explicitly state this association was "largely attenuated after adjusting for renal function," caution against using homocysteine as a standalone prognostic tool, and the cross-sectional design cannot establish causality or temporality [Lin et al., 2026 (homocysteine)].

A newly identified tier-1 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 9 retrospective cohorts (n=1,185 kidney transplant recipients with recurrent/transplant IgA nephropathy) to assess the Oxford MEST-C classification's prognostic utility for allograft loss — a kidney-transplant/allograft-recurrence population that is kept distinct from, and not merged with, the native-kidney IgAN population discussed above [Ștefan et al., 2023] or the IgA vasculitis nephritis population discussed below [Barbour et al., 2024]. S, T, and C lesions were consistently associated with allograft loss across the pooled cohorts, whereas M and E lesions and the total MEST-C score showed inconsistent or non-independent associations once multivariable pooling was applied; the authors themselves state that the total MEST-C score's "clinical utility requires robust prospective validation before routine reporting can be recommended" [Rath et al., 2026]. Although classified as tier 1 on the study-type ladder, all 9 pooled cohorts were themselves retrospective with heterogeneous study-specific multivariable models, and the available abstract/metadata text did not report a formal risk-of-bias tool, a publication-bias assessment, or funding/COI disclosures; specific pooled hazard ratios, confidence intervals, and p-values per MEST-C component were not extractable from the available text, since the full article (a subscriber-only Oxford Academic advance-access publication) could not be obtained — extraction for this row is abstract/metadata-level only. **A notable, unresolved pattern worth surfacing rather than resolving:** each of the three MEST-C populations represented in this evidence base finds a different component subset to be the "robust" prognostic one in its own population — crescents/complement activation in native-kidney IgAN [Ștefan et al., 2023], endocapillary hypercellularity (E1) alone in IgA vasculitis nephritis [Barbour et al., 2024, discussed under IgAV Diagnostics below], and segmental sclerosis/tubular atrophy-interstitial fibrosis/crescents (S/T/C) in transplant/allograft-recurrence IgAN [Rath et al., 2026]. This cross-population divergence is not explained by anything in this evidence base and should not be over-interpreted as a contradiction to be resolved here.

A further newly identified study examines a related but distinct question in this same broad transplant-IgAN population: whether crescents on the **native** (pre-transplant) kidney biopsy predict IgAN **recurrence** after kidney transplantation, as opposed to Rath et al.'s finding above that MEST-C lesions scored on the **allograft** biopsy predict allograft **loss**. A single-center retrospective cohort (n=63 kidney-transplant recipients with biopsy-confirmed native-kidney IgAN, Lausanne University Hospital, transplanted 2003–2019) found no significant association between native-kidney crescents and IgAN recurrence in the renal allograft (22.2% recurrence overall; median post-transplant follow-up 7.4 years). A meta-analysis pooling this cohort with 5 further cohorts (total n=311 across 6 cohorts, 23% with recurrent IgAN overall) confirmed the null finding: pooled RR for recurrence 1.53 (95% CI 0.40–5.90, p=.41), with the same null result in the subgroup of 3 cohorts using a MEST-C-based crescent definition. The authors conclude that glomerular crescents — especially when present in ≥25% of viable glomeruli — remain an unfavorable prognostic factor for progression of native-kidney IgAN to kidney failure, but do not appear to represent a risk factor for IgAN recurrence in the allograft after transplantation [Guzzo et al., 2026]. The authors themselves explicitly state that this null result may reflect low statistical power rather than a true absence of association (moderate heterogeneity, I²=72.09%; a borderline publication-bias signal, Egger's p=.08), and note heterogeneous crescent definitions across the 6 pooled cohorts, a retrospective design with possible selection bias, and possible confounding by changes in immunosuppression protocols over the two-decade-plus study span; the wide confidence interval around the pooled RR means a clinically meaningful association cannot be confidently excluded. **This is a different exposure and outcome from Rath et al. 2026 discussed immediately above, not a conflicting finding: Guzzo et al. examines crescents on the native pre-transplant biopsy as a predictor of IgAN recurrence in the allograft, whereas Rath et al. examines MEST-C lesions scored on the allograft biopsy itself (in an already-transplanted/recurrent-IgAN population) as a predictor of allograft loss (a structural/functional graft-survival endpoint, any cause). The two findings sit in overlapping but non-identical transplant-IgAN populations, measure different exposures (native vs. allograft biopsy) and different outcomes (recurrence vs. graft loss), and are presented here as a non-contradictory, complementary pattern — not a conflict requiring resolution** [Guzzo et al., 2026; Rath et al., 2026].

A separate single-center retrospective cohort study (n=180 biopsy-proven IgAN patients with ≥3 years of clinical follow-up, China) modeled patient-specific eGFR trajectory, finding a median eGFR slope of −1.12 mL/min/1.73m²/year and 19.4% of patients (35/180) meeting an operational rapid-progression threshold (<−5 mL/min/1.73m²/year). In the primary multivariable model, baseline albuminuria (ln[UACR+1]) was the most consistent predictor of steeper decline (β=−0.612, 95% CI −1.073 to −0.150), and Oxford MEST-C T2 lesions predicted 3.87 mL/min/1.73m²/year faster loss versus T0 (β=−3.870, 95% CI −7.087 to −0.654), while T1 did not differ from T0; overall model fit was modest (R²=0.159, adjusted R²=0.130) [Lin et al., 2026 (eGFR-slope cohort)]. The study authors themselves characterize the T2-lesion finding as "tentative" and "strictly hypothesis-generating" rather than confirmed: it derived from only 16 of 180 patients (8.9%) and lost statistical significance when the analysis was restricted to patients with ≥5 eGFR measurements, whereas baseline albuminuria remained the most consistent adverse marker across sensitivity analyses. This is a single-center, retrospective study with no treatment comparator; the modest model fit leaves most eGFR-slope variance unexplained, the ≥3-year-follow-up inclusion requirement may introduce survivorship/selection bias against rapidly progressing or lost-to-follow-up patients, and the single Chinese-population center limits generalizability. The instability of the Oxford T2 association under sensitivity analysis is a useful, study-specific caveat on MEST-C-based prognostication in IgAN and should not be read as undermining MEST-C's broader prognostic role described elsewhere in this table [Ștefan et al., 2023; Jaugey et al., 2023; Hölscher et al., 2026], only as a reminder that individual lesion-category associations can be fragile in smaller single-center samples.

A separate multicenter retrospective cohort (n=788 adults with biopsy-proven IgAN, diagnosed 2002–2021, median follow-up 70 months) examined hemoglobin level at biopsy as a sex-stratified predictor of progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), using a defined reference band (Hb 12.0–12.9 g/dL) as the comparator. ESKD occurred in 8.4% (66/788) of patients over follow-up; hemoglobin <11.0 g/dL was independently associated with higher ESKD risk overall (adjusted HR 2.88, 95% CI 1.24–6.68, p=0.014), with a nonlinear relationship showing risk rising markedly below approximately 12 g/dL. In sex-stratified analysis, both Hb <11.0 g/dL (aHR 5.10, 95% CI 1.35–19.29, p=0.016) and Hb 11.0–11.9 g/dL (aHR 4.66, 95% CI 1.26–17.19, p=0.021) were significantly associated with ESKD risk in women, whereas no significant association was found in men; the study authors conclude that hemoglobin may serve as "a simple and clinically useful prognostic marker" for risk stratification in IgAN [Yoo et al., 2026]. This is a tier-4 multicenter retrospective cohort with a defined comparator band — a methodological strength — but the authors' own stated limitations and funding/COI disclosures were not extractable from the available abstract-only text (full text subscriber-only via the publisher). Several further limitations should be weighed in interpreting this finding: the abstract does not specify which covariates (e.g., baseline eGFR, proteinuria) were entered into the Cox models, so the degree of control for reverse causation or confounding by underlying CKD severity cannot be assessed from the available text; the null finding in men may reflect an underpowered subgroup, since only 66 total ESKD events were split across two sexes and five hemoglobin bands, and point estimates/confidence intervals for the male stratum were not reported in the abstract — this should not be read as evidence of equivalence between sexes; and the two-decade diagnosis window (2002–2021) raises a possible era effect from changes in background RAAS-blockade/SGLT2-inhibitor therapy over that period, which is not addressed in the available text.

A two-center retrospective diagnostic-accuracy cohort with a defined comparator (non-IgAN kidney disease patients — membranous nephropathy, FSGS, and other biopsy-confirmed glomerular disease — plus healthy controls) evaluated an ELISA-based test kit for circulating polymeric IgA immune complexes (Poly-IgA) as a candidate diagnostic biomarker for IgAN, finding an AUC of 0.87 and, at an optimal cutoff of 75.6 U/mL, sensitivity 82.7%, specificity 76.0%, and overall accuracy 78.0% for differentiating IgAN from non-IgAN kidney disease [Wang et al., 2026]. The study authors themselves state that a specificity of 76% is "insufficient to replace kidney biopsy as the diagnostic gold standard" and explicitly flag their "relatively small sample size," calling for prospective multicenter validation in larger, more diverse populations; the full text was paywalled, so extraction was limited to the abstract, and exact per-group sample sizes were not extractable from the available text.

A separate, small (n=6) retrospective clinicopathological case series from a Tunisian referral center describes minimal change disease with dominant/exclusive mesangial IgA deposition on immunofluorescence but minimal-change or near-minimal light-microscopy findings — an entity the authors themselves situate "at the interface between MCD and IgAN" and regard as nosologically unsettled, proposing three competing (unresolved) pathogenic hypotheses: incidental IgA trapping, a dual glomerulopathy with subclinical IgAN, or a unified pathogenesis with shared lymphocyte dysregulation [Hajji et al., 2026]. Steroid-sensitive/dependent relapsing courses were common across the six cases, with electron microscopy unavailable throughout the study period and no Gd-IgA1 biomarker analysis performed to confirm pathogenic IgA. This is an n=6 case series carrying explicit low evidentiary weight under `research-question.md`'s tier-7 diagnostic-pitfall carve-out — included specifically because the authors themselves frame correct recognition of this entity as important "to avoid diagnostic misclassification and inappropriate therapeutic strategies" relative to classical proliferative IgAN, and must not be generalized to typical proliferative IgAN or weighted as independent confirmatory evidence.

A newly identified single-center retrospective cohort (n=105 biopsy-proven primary IgAN patients, China) classified patients by glomerular light-chain deposition pattern — κ-dominant (n=21), κ-λ co-deposition (n=42), or λ-dominant (n=42), matched 1:2:2 on age/sex/24-hour proteinuria/baseline creatinine — and found that renal progression-free survival (sustained ≥30% rise in serum creatinine; only 21 total progression events across the cohort) differed significantly across the three groups (log-rank p=0.0046); in the adjusted multivariable model, a higher κ-deposition ratio was independently associated with lower hazard of renal progression (HR 0.96 per 1-percentage-point increase, 95% CI 0.94–0.99, p=0.011) over a median follow-up of 25 months. The full text additionally reports that IgG co-deposition may represent a distinct immune-complex phenotype potentially linked to increased IgG-containing immune complexes and complement activation, which the authors propose as a candidate subgroup for future complement-targeted-therapy trials, while explicitly cautioning that glomerular IgG deposition "alone cannot be used as a validated marker to predict response to complement inhibitors" [Liu et al., 2026 (κ/λ light-chain deposition)]. The authors themselves state this is a single-center, retrospective study with a small κ-dominant subgroup (n=21, reflecting the rarity of this phenotype) and correspondingly few progression events, that IgG-subclass staining was not available despite electron microscopy being performed in all patients (limiting mechanistic interpretation of the IgG co-deposition finding), and that the ≥30% creatinine-rise endpoint used is "less definitive than established hard renal outcomes such as doubling of serum creatinine, kidney failure, or the need for kidney replacement therapy"; the authors explicitly characterize their findings as exploratory, requiring validation in larger multicenter cohorts with longer follow-up and established renal endpoints. This single-country, relatively short (25-month median)-follow-up study should be read as hypothesis-generating evidence for a candidate histopathologic prognostic marker, not a validated one, and funding/conflict-of-interest disclosures were not extractable from the available full text.

#### Treatment

As background, a single-author narrative review frames current IgAN management as resting on foundation therapy (RAAS blockade, SGLT2 inhibitors) plus an expanding set of approved or late-stage targeted agents (budesonide, iptacopan, endothelin receptor antagonists, BAFF/APRIL inhibitors), while concluding that validated predictive biomarkers and combination-therapy data remain lacking [Jiang, 2026, background]. The primary trial evidence substantiating and qualifying that targeted-therapy landscape follows below.

**Endothelin receptor antagonists and endothelin/angiotensin dual blockade.** The PROTECT phase 3 trial of sparsentan (a dual endothelin/angiotensin receptor antagonist) versus active-comparator irbesartan in adults with IgAN (n=404) is represented by three linked reports of the same underlying trial maturing over time, not three independent confirmations: the prespecified interim analysis found a 41% relative reduction in urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) versus irbesartan at week 36 (p<0.0001) [Heerspink et al., 2023]; the 2-year (110-week) final results found a sustained 40% lower UPCR and an eGFR-slope benefit of 1.1 mL/min/1.73m²/year (p=0.037), but the composite kidney-failure endpoint numerically favored sparsentan (9% vs. 13%) without reaching statistical significance (RR 0.7, 95% CI 0.4–1.2) — an important null hard-outcome finding that should not be smoothed over by the significant surrogate results [Rovin et al., 2023]; and a post hoc analysis found that achieving complete proteinuria remission (irrespective of original treatment arm) was associated with better eGFR preservation and fewer kidney-failure events, supporting proteinuria as a surrogate endpoint, though this was a non-randomized comparison by remission status carrying substantial confounding-by-indication risk [Heerspink et al., 2025/2026]. All three PROTECT reports used an active comparator (irbesartan), not placebo, and are sponsor-funded (Travere Therapeutics); a preclinical gddY-mouse study by partly overlapping industry-affiliated authors found sparsentan superior to losartan in reducing albuminuria and glomerulosclerosis, offering mechanistic support for PROTECT's clinical findings but remaining preclinical evidence [Nagasawa et al., 2024].

The selective endothelin-A antagonist atrasentan was tested in a separate, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial, ALIGN (final 2.5-year results, n=404 randomized): the key secondary eGFR endpoint at week 136 narrowly missed the prespecified significance threshold (2.4 mL/min/1.73m² difference, 95% CI −0.1 to 4.8, p=0.057) — an important near-null finding not to be overstated — although the total eGFR slope difference (1.4 mL/min/1.73m²/year, 95% CI 0.5–2.3) favored atrasentan, and an exploratory SGLT2-inhibitor-background stratum (n=64) showed a larger eGFR difference (9.1 mL/min/1.73m², 95% CI 3.0–15.2), a hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory finding [Heerspink et al., 2026, ALIGN]. A separate, smaller placebo-controlled crossover trial (n=54) in patients already on background RASi+SGLT2i therapy found atrasentan reduced UPCR by 25.3% versus placebo at week 12 (95% CI −36.8 to −11.7, p<0.001), though this was a small trial with short (12-week) treatment periods [Heerspink et al., 2026, atrasentan+SGLT2i]. A separate, open-label, academically-run 3-arm crossover trial (n=65, no pharmaceutical funding disclosed) compared ambrisentan, the SGLT2 inhibitor henagliflozin, and their combination, finding the combination reduced UPCR similarly to ambrisentan alone (−44% vs. −48%) but significantly more than henagliflozin alone (−21%), with less fluid retention than ERA monotherapy — but the open-label design and 4-week treatment arms are substantial limitations [Chen et al., 2026].

**Targeted-release formulation (TRF) budesonide (Nefecon).** The NefIgArd phase 3, placebo-controlled trial is represented by two reports of the same trial: Part A (9-month primary endpoint, n=199) found UPCR 27% lower versus placebo [Barratt et al., 2022, NefIgArd Part A], and the 2-year final results (n=364) found the primary endpoint (time-weighted average eGFR) favored Nefecon by 5.05 mL/min/1.73m² (95% CI 3.24–7.38, p<0.0001) — one of the strongest effect sizes in this evidence base — but with a meaningful adverse-event burden (peripheral oedema 17% vs. 4%, hypertension 12% vs. 3%, muscle spasms 12% vs. 4%, acne 11% vs. 1%, Nefecon vs. placebo), a safety/tolerability tradeoff that belongs alongside the efficacy finding in any risk-benefit synthesis [Lafayette et al., 2023, NefIgArd 2-year]. A separate real-world, single-center, retrospective observational case series of 45 consecutive Asian-American adults with IgAN treated with Nefecon at a US clinic found eGFR broadly stable overall (mean change +0.9 mL/min/1.73m²) and UPCR reduced by 19% on average, with a more pronounced benefit (26% UPCR reduction, eGFR preservation) in the subgroup completing the labeled ≥9-month treatment duration versus a decline in eGFR and only a 3.3% UPCR reduction in those treated <9 months; the authors explicitly caution that this duration-subgroup comparison reflects differential treatment persistence and confounding by indication, not a randomized exposure contrast, and "cannot be taken as evidence that longer treatment duration per se causes better outcomes" [Sun et al., 2026]. Adverse events occurred in 16% (7/45), with an 11% discontinuation rate comparable to NefIgArd's. **This study carries a notable conflict-of-interest flag beyond the typical sponsor-funding disclosures elsewhere in this table: it was funded by Calliditas Therapeutics (Nefecon's manufacturer, now part of Asahi Kasei Corporation), and several co-authors were Calliditas employees during the study or received direct Calliditas funding for study design, conduct, analysis, and medical-writing support** — a materially higher level of industry entanglement than the sponsor-funded-but-independently-conducted pattern seen elsewhere in this evidence base, and this should be weighted accordingly when interpreting its real-world findings as corroborating NefIgArd. A separate short research-letter-format report describes TRF-budesonide use in recurrent IgAN after kidney transplantation, a population outside NefIgArd's inclusion criteria [Gandolfini et al., 2023]. **This record's classification as a data-bearing research letter (rather than a non-data commentary) is now MODERATE confidence, upgraded from LOW**: PubMed still lists it as "Letter"/"Comment" with no abstract, and the primary full text remains inaccessible after eleven independent verification paths were tried, but a targeted follow-up found that this letter pairs with "the authors reply" from NefIgArd's own senior investigators (Barratt & Rovin), consistent with a substantive data-engaging exchange rather than pure commentary — though this still falls short of HIGH confidence since the primary text itself remains unread. **Per an independent citing source (PMC12630289), and NOT independently verified against Gandolfini et al.'s own text:** that citing paper paraphrases Gandolfini et al. as having analyzed approximately 10 kidney-transplant recipients with recurrent IgAN and found no benefit from TRF-budesonide beyond the corticosteroids these patients were already receiving for allograft-rejection prevention. The citing paper additionally offers its own explanation for this null result — a different (whole-colon-release) formulation and a lower dose/shorter duration than in its own reported case — but that explanation is the *citing author's* interpretation, not a limitation stated by Gandolfini et al. themselves, whose primary text remains unread. This secondary-source-derived description is uncorroborated by any second independent citing source and must not be treated with the same evidentiary weight as a directly-extracted finding elsewhere in this table; it should be read as a low-confidence lead consistent with no observed benefit in a small post-transplant case series, not as a confirmed result.

A newly identified single-institution, retrospective, single-arm case series (n=12 treatment-naïve primary IgAN patients of 20 eligible, China) examined the combined and simultaneous use of Nefecon (16 mg/day) plus irbesartan (150 mg/day, fixed dose) from treatment initiation over 6 months. Mean 24-hour urinary protein fell from baseline by 28.28% at 1 month, 32.78% at 3 months, and 51.46% at 6 months (all p<.001), eGFR rose significantly by 3 and 6 months (p=.009), and urinary dysmorphic red blood cells decreased markedly by 6 months; an exploratory, underpowered Gd-IgA1 correlation (r=0.664, p=.018, n=12, measured only at baseline and 1 month) and an exploratory sex-based subgroup difference (p=.037, comparing only 6 vs. 6 patients) were also reported [Tian et al., 2026 (Nefecon + irbesartan)]. **The study authors themselves explicitly state that, as a retrospective, single-arm study without a monotherapy or untreated comparator, "we cannot determine whether the observed improvements reflect an additive benefit of the combination, the effect of either agent alone, natural fluctuation of proteinuria, or regression to the mean."** They further caveat that the 8 of 20 eligible patients excluded "may have introduced selection bias, although a qualitative comparison showed no marked baseline differences," that the sex-based and Gd-IgA1-correlation subgroup analyses were underpowered, and that results are "exploratory and require confirmation in controlled studies." This is a very low-evidentiary-weight, uncontrolled pilot (n=12) that is directionally consistent with — but does not independently establish an added effect specific to the irbesartan combination beyond — the placebo-controlled NefIgArd RCT evidence for Nefecon monotherapy above [Barratt et al., 2022, NefIgArd Part A; Lafayette et al., 2023, NefIgArd 2-year] and the real-world Nefecon case series also discussed above [Sun et al., 2026]; funding/conflict-of-interest disclosures were not located in the retrieved full text.

**Complement pathway inhibitors.** Iptacopan (an alternative complement pathway/Factor B inhibitor) has the most mature complement-pathway evidence stream in this table, again representing one trial program at different timepoints, not independent confirmations: a placebo-controlled phase 2 dose-finding trial (n=112) supported progression to phase 3 without itself reporting detailed quantitative efficacy in the available record [Zhang et al., 2023]; the phase 3 APPLAUSE-IgAN 9-month interim analysis (n=443) found a 38.3% greater UPCR reduction versus placebo (95% CI 26.0–48.6, p<0.001) [Perkovic et al., 2024]; and the APPLAUSE-IgAN final 24-month analysis (n=477) found the primary annualized total eGFR slope endpoint favored iptacopan (−3.10 vs. −6.12 mL/min/1.73m²/year, difference 3.02, p<0.001) and — notably — a statistically significant reduction in the composite kidney-failure endpoint (21.4% vs. 33.5%, HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.40–0.81, p=0.003), one of the few IgAN RCTs in this evidence base with a significant hard clinical outcome rather than only a surrogate; this benefit came with a counterbalancing safety signal of numerically higher serious infections (6.7% vs. 2.1%) that should be weighed alongside the efficacy result, not omitted [Barratt et al., 2026, APPLAUSE-IgAN final]. A design/rationale paper for APPLAUSE-IgAN reports no outcomes and is cited only for methodological context [Rizk et al., 2023]. Two further complement-pathway agents — not among the three drugs explicitly named in `research-question.md` (iptacopan/avacopan/narsoplimab) but included under the same-mechanism-class judgment call applied consistently across this table — showed positive phase 2 signals: ravulizumab (complement C5 inhibitor, n=66) reduced proteinuria by 30.1% relative to placebo at week 26 (p=0.005) with stable eGFR through week 26, though eGFR declined further in both arms during an open-label extension to week 50, a pattern warranting caution about durability, and a phase 3 trial is only now enrolling [Lafayette et al., 2025]; cemdisiran (an RNAi therapeutic targeting complement C5, n=31, placebo arm n=9 only) reduced placebo-adjusted UPCR by 37.4% at week 32, though the very small placebo arm substantially limits precision [Barratt et al., 2024, cemdisiran].

That ravulizumab phase 2 signal has now matured into a newly identified, larger, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial — a distinct, later program ("I CAN," NCT06291376), not a duplicate of the phase 2 trial above (NCT04564339). In a prespecified interim analysis (485 randomized, interim population n=352), ravulizumab reduced 24-hour UPCR from baseline by a geometric mean of 46.6% versus 5.6% with placebo at week 34 (adjusted geometric-mean difference 43.4% lower with ravulizumab, 99% CI 30.1–54.2, p<0.001); adverse events occurred in 69.5% (ravulizumab) versus 75.8% (placebo), serious adverse events in 4.6% versus 9.0%, with no meningococcal infections or deaths in either arm [Barratt et al., 2026 (ravulizumab phase 3 interim, "I CAN")]. **This is explicitly a prespecified INTERIM analysis reporting only a surrogate (proteinuria) endpoint — eGFR-slope and kidney-failure (hard-outcome) data are not yet reported and would require a later publication from this same trial, analogous to how the APPLAUSE-IgAN final analysis followed its own interim analysis above.** The trial is sponsor-funded (Alexion, AstraZeneca Rare Disease Unit); full text was paywalled (no PMC record; NEJM DOI fetch returned HTTP 403), so extraction is abstract/metadata-level only, with detailed safety-signal breakdowns and subgroup analyses not extractable from the available text.

As further background, a 2025 International Society of Nephrology Forum report characterized complement as a secondary (rather than primary, as in C3 glomerulopathy) pathogenic driver in IgAN, and discussed how complement biomarkers, autoantibodies, genetics, and biopsy findings could help guide use of complement-inhibitor therapy in this disease [Kavanagh et al., 2026, background — IgAN-specific content only; this report's substantive C3 glomerulopathy content concerns a distinct, out-of-scope disease and is not drawn upon here]. A newly identified preclinical study extends the complement-pathway therapeutic-development evidence base with a structurally distinct candidate: a GalNAc-conjugated small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting complement component C5, tested in vitro (IC50 = 0.013 nM) and in a cynomolgus-monkey IgAN model. A single subcutaneous injection produced sustained C5 suppression exceeding 80% for 2 months and exceeding 50% for up to 4 months; in a head-to-head comparison against Nefecon (TRF-budesonide, already discussed above) in the same monkey IgAN model, both agents produced comparable improvements in renal-function parameters, but the C5-siRNA achieved superior pathological remission and near-complete blockade of glomerular C5 deposition and membrane-attack-complex formation [Zhang et al., 2026, C5-siRNA]. This is preclinical, non-human-primate evidence only, with no human clinical data yet; several authors are affiliated with the siRNA's likely industry developer (a notable conflict-of-interest-adjacent detail, though a formal disclosure statement was not extractable from the abstract), and the "superior to Nefecon" framing rests on a single preclinical comparison and should not be extrapolated to imply clinical superiority in humans. It is mechanistically adjacent to, but structurally distinct (RNA interference versus small-molecule/antibody/fusion-protein) from, every complement-pathway agent already in human trials in this evidence base (iptacopan, ravulizumab, cemdisiran).

**No dedicated IgAN or IgAV trial for avacopan or narsoplimab was found despite explicit, saturated searching.** For avacopan, three consecutive reformulated Consensus queries returned zero new in-scope candidates — all hits were for the distinct diseases ANCA-associated vasculitis or C3 glomerulopathy — confirming this as a genuine evidence gap as of the search date, not an artifact of insufficient searching [per `sources/search-log.md`, Runs 11–13]. For narsoplimab, only case reports and trial-design-only conference abstracts were found; a citing commentary suggested the pivotal phase 3 trial may have missed its primary endpoint, but this was not independently verified as a primary source and is therefore not usable as a finding — it is recorded here as an unresolved gap, not a conclusion [per `sources/search-log.md`, Run 10 notes].

**BAFF/APRIL-targeted therapies.** Sibeprenlimab (an APRIL-neutralizing monoclonal antibody, included under the same-mechanism-class judgment call since it is not one of `research-question.md`'s three explicitly named BAFF/APRIL drugs) showed a dose-dependent proteinuria reduction of 47.2%–62.0% versus 20.0% with placebo at 12 months in a phase 2 trial (n=155), with eGFR stabilization in higher-dose arms versus decline with placebo [Mathur et al., 2023, ENVISION]. Atacicept, one of the three explicitly named drugs, was tested in two trials representing an escalating evidence stream: an earlier, very small (n=16) phase II precursor (JANUS) found an early pharmacodynamic (Gd-IgA1 reduction) signal without being powered for efficacy conclusions [Barratt et al., 2022, JANUS], and the larger phase 2b ORIGIN trial (n=116) met its primary endpoint with a 25% relative UPCR reduction versus placebo at week 24, eGFR stabilization versus decline with placebo, and a 60% reduction in Gd-IgA1 versus placebo as mechanistic corroboration [Lafayette et al., 2024, ORIGIN]. Telitacicept, another explicitly named drug, showed a dose-dependent and partly negative controlled-trial result: in a placebo-controlled phase 2 trial (n=44), the 240mg dose reduced proteinuria by 49% from baseline versus placebo (p=0.013), but the 160mg dose was not statistically significant (p=0.389) — both doses' results should be represented together, not just the positive one [Lv et al., 2022]. Separately, a small retrospective, uncontrolled case series of adolescents receiving telitacicept reported combined-cohort (IgAN n=11 + IgAVN n=4, pooled n=15) proteinuria reductions of 31.2% at 3 months and 59.0% at 12 months with stable eGFR, but IgAN-specific stratified results were not extractable from the available abstract — the quoted effect sizes apply only to the pooled cohort, and this is a small, retrospective, single-arm series with no comparator [Wu et al., 2025, IgAN arm]. A further small, single-center, retrospective, self-controlled before-after case series (n=12) of IgAN patients on stable background ACEI/ARB+SGLT2i therapy receiving add-on telitacicept for 6 months reported significant reductions in 24-hour urinary protein at 3 and 6 months (75.0% clinical remission rate), eGFR improvement in 83.3% of patients, and hematuria remission in 70.0% of those with baseline hematuria, with a low, mild-only adverse-event rate [Gu et al., 2026]. This uncontrolled, self-controlled before-after design, with no placebo or active-comparator arm, cannot rule out regression-to-the-mean or background-therapy co-effects as alternative explanations, and should be read as real-world evidence directionally consistent with — but not independent confirmation of — the controlled telitacicept RCT above [Lv et al., 2022].

A newly identified placebo-controlled phase 3 trial adds povetacicept — a dual BAFF/APRIL inhibitor (an enhanced TACI-Fc fusion protein) not itself among `research-question.md`'s three named BAFF/APRIL drugs (telitacicept/atacicept/blisibimod) but included under the same general "BAFF/APRIL inhibitors" mechanism-category judgment call applied elsewhere in this table — to the BAFF/APRIL evidence stream. In a prespecified interim analysis of the RAINIER trial (NCT06564142; 557 dosed, efficacy analysis population n=199 of those first randomized), povetacicept reduced 24-hour UPCR by 52.0% versus 4.3% with placebo at week 36 (adjusted difference −49.8 percentage points, 95% CI −58.6 to −39.2, p<0.001); Gd-IgA1 fell 77.4% with povetacicept versus a 9.1% increase with placebo (adjusted difference −79.3 points, 95% CI −82.2 to −75.9, p<0.001); and hematuria resolution among those with baseline hematuria occurred in 85% versus 23% with placebo (adjusted difference 62 points, 95% CI 45–78, p<0.001); adverse events were similar between arms (75% vs. 78%; serious adverse events 3% vs. 4%) [Barratt et al., 2026 (povetacicept phase 3 interim, RAINIER)]. **As with ravulizumab's phase 3 interim analysis above, this is explicitly a prespecified INTERIM analysis on surrogate/biomarker endpoints only (proteinuria, Gd-IgA1, hematuria resolution) — no eGFR-slope or kidney-failure hard-outcome data have been reported at this stage.** The efficacy analysis population (n=199) is considerably smaller than the total dosed population (n=557), reflecting staggered/ongoing enrollment at this interim cut rather than attrition — meaning the reported effect sizes are based on well under half of currently-dosed participants. The trial is sponsor-funded (Vertex Pharmaceuticals); full text was paywalled (no PMC record; NEJM DOI fetch returned HTTP 403), so extraction is abstract/metadata-level only, with author-stated limitations and subgroup analyses not extractable from the available text.

As further background, the BAFF/APRIL pathway's pathogenic rationale and expanding therapeutic landscape in IgAN (sibeprenlimab, zigakibart, atacicept, povetacicept, telitacicept) has been summarized narratively elsewhere [Vasquez-Rios et al., 2026, background]. Within that landscape, atacicept reached a regulatory milestone during this review period: a drug-approval-summary background source reports that atacicept received US accelerated approval (7 July 2026) for proteinuria reduction in adults with primary IgAN at risk of progression, based on the ORIGIN/JANUS trial data already discussed above [Blair, 2026, background; cf. Lafayette et al., 2024, ORIGIN; Barratt et al., 2022, JANUS] — this is a regulatory-milestone note, not new trial evidence, and the underlying efficacy data should continue to be cited via the primary ORIGIN/JANUS records, not via this secondary summary.

A newly identified real-world, propensity-score-matched retrospective cohort (n=206 biopsy-proven IgAN, Tongji Hospital, China; 69 belimumab-treated vs. 137 matched standard-therapy controls) adds belimumab — a BLyS/BAFF-targeted monoclonal antibody, included under the same BAFF/APRIL-inhibitor mechanism-class judgment call applied elsewhere in this table — to the BAFF/APRIL evidence stream. Belimumab was associated with significantly higher proteinuria-remission rates than matched standard therapy at 12 months (93% vs. 80%, P=.025), with the gap already apparent at 3 months (59% vs. 46%) and 6 months (77% vs. 66%); in newly diagnosed patients specifically, remission was significantly higher with belimumab at 3 months (76.5% vs. 47.1%, P=.009) and 6 months (88.2% vs. 67.6%, P=.045) [Wu et al., 2026, belimumab]. **However, the corresponding time-averaged UPCR difference — a continuous measure of the same underlying outcome domain — did NOT reach statistical significance at 12 months (371 vs. 463 mg/g at 6 months; 360 vs. 433 mg/g at 12 months, P=.066), a discordant result that should be presented together with the significant remission-rate finding, not reported selectively.** This is a retrospective, single-center, propensity-score-matched (not randomized) design that reduces but does not eliminate confounding by indication, since why belimumab was chosen for a given patient is not described in the available abstract; the study authors' own conclusion that belimumab "may represent a superior therapeutic approach" overstates what an observational design of this kind can support, and this real-world evidence sits lower in the evidentiary hierarchy than the placebo-controlled interim RCTs already described above for other BAFF/APRIL agents and should not be weighted equivalently in synthesis.

**B-cell-depleting therapy (rituximab).** Rituximab, a CD20-targeted B-cell-depleting agent explicitly named in `research-question.md`'s intervention scope, now has its first directly-verified RCT in this evidence base. A newly identified multicenter randomized controlled trial ("RITA," 106 Chinese adults across 10 centers, 53 per arm) tested rituximab added to RASi therapy, versus RASi therapy alone, in IgAN patients with 24-hour urine total protein >1 g/day despite ≥3 months of RASi therapy. The primary endpoint (24-hour urine total protein change at month 12) showed no significant difference: adjusted geometric mean ratio 1.05 (95% CI 0.80–1.36); median change −0.21 g/day (rituximab) versus −0.26 g/day (RASi alone). Secondary endpoints were likewise non-significant: eGFR (adjusted mean difference 3.80 mL/min/1.73m², 95% CI −0.47 to 8.06), serum Gd-IgA1 (adjusted GMR 1.04, 95% CI 0.87–1.24), and the proportion achieving ≥50% proteinuria reduction (15.1% vs. 20.8%). No kidney-failure-related hard-outcome events occurred in either arm during the 12-month treatment period, adverse events occurred at similar rates between groups, and findings were consistent with exploratory analyses incorporating outcome data through month 24. The study authors' own conclusion: "No additional reduction in proteinuria or preservation of kidney function was observed with the addition of RTX to optimized RASI-based therapy" [Ouyang et al., 2026, RITA]. **This is the first rituximab RCT for IgAN directly included in this evidence table — distinct from, not the same study as, the external RCT that two already-included studies cite secondhand when discussing rituximab in IgAN** [Gentile et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2024] **(see Pathogenesis above); those citations predate RITA's 2026 publication and cannot be referring to it.** RITA's own null finding is directionally consistent with that previously secondhand-cited external RCT's reported lack of proteinuria/renal-function benefit — a pattern across two different trials that modestly strengthens, without proving, the broader picture that B-cell depletion via rituximab alone has not demonstrated efficacy in native-kidney IgAN, in contrast to this table's positive complement-pathway [Barratt et al., 2026, APPLAUSE-IgAN final] and real-world BAFF/APRIL-pathway [Wu et al., 2026, belimumab] findings discussed above — a mechanism-class contrast flagged for interpretation, not resolved here. The trial authors themselves note the follow-up period was relatively short and only Asian patients were enrolled; funding was government/academic/non-profit, with no pharmaceutical sponsor disclosed — a notable contrast to most other IgAN RCTs in this table. Full text was not obtained (no PMC record; a DOI fetch redirected to a paywalled Elsevier page), though the abstract itself is unusually complete; randomization method, blinding status, and specific per-arm adverse-event/infection counts beyond "similar rates" were not extractable from the available text.

A separate, newly identified single case report illustrates a novel rescue approach for rituximab-refractory disease in a more severe, distinct population. A 17-year-old kidney-transplant recipient with biopsy-confirmed recurrent crescentic IgAN, refractory to corticosteroids and targeted-release budesonide, received one rituximab infusion combined with daratumumab (a CD38-targeted monoclonal antibody), followed by four further daratumumab infusions. Proteinuria fell from 5.21 to 0.06 g/g, serum albumin normalized, and allograft function remained stable through day 482 after the first rescue infusion; longitudinal immune-cell monitoring showed sustained peripheral B-cell depletion and transient NK-cell reductions, and sequential biopsies showed resolution of the active cellular crescent, though mesangial IgA staining and electron-dense deposits persisted [Zhang et al., 2026, rituximab+daratumumab]. The study authors themselves state explicitly that "this single case cannot establish efficacy, distinguish the contributions of rituximab and daratumumab, or exclude effects of preceding and supportive treatments, but supports further study of this rescue approach." This is an n=1 case report with no comparator and no statistical inference possible, included via the tier-7 "genuinely novel or emerging intervention" carve-out because combined CD20+CD38-targeted rescue therapy is not previously represented in this evidence base; it addresses a distinct, more severe population (transplant-recurrent, crescentic IgAN) than RITA's native-kidney, non-crescentic population above, and than the rituximab-in-IgAVN evidence discussed under IgA Vasculitis Treatment below [Xiong et al., 2024], so it is read as a population difference rather than conflicting evidence, and must not be weighted as independent confirmatory evidence of either drug's efficacy.

**Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism.** A single-center, retrospective, single-arm case series (n=52) of adults initiating finerenone found a 29.7% reduction in 24-hour urinary protein by month 12 (p=0.003), with complete or partial remission in roughly a third of eligible patients and stable eGFR; the authors themselves note that finerenone evidence in IgAN specifically remains limited and is largely extrapolated from diabetic kidney disease, and this uncontrolled, 12-month, single-center series is hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory [Pei et al., 2026]. A newly identified multicentre retrospective cohort (n=258 after 1:1:1 propensity-score matching, 86/group) examined finerenone specifically in the clinical scenario of continuing versus discontinuing RASi: at 12 months, urinary total protein (UTP) fell similarly with finerenone-alone (after RASi discontinuation, 32.24%) and RASi-alone (34.35%, p=.220, not significant), but the finerenone+RASi combination achieved a significantly greater UTP reduction (54.43%) than either monotherapy (both p<0.01), with correspondingly higher Kaplan-Meier "significant response" rates (both log-rank p=.001) [Zeng et al., 2026]. **Notably, this greater proteinuria reduction with combination therapy did not translate into a better eGFR-slope outcome: the combination group showed a numerically (though not statistically significantly) steeper total eGFR decline than either monotherapy (−1.48 vs. −0.44 [finerenone-alone] vs. −0.52 [RASi-alone] mL/min/1.73m²/yr) — a tension the evidence table flags explicitly rather than resolving, possibly reflecting underpowering for the eGFR-slope comparison specifically rather than a true adverse signal, though this cannot be confirmed from the abstract alone.** This is a retrospective design despite propensity-score matching, abstract-level extraction only, and — together with the Pei et al. 2026 case series above — constitutes the entirety of the mineralocorticoid-receptor-antagonist evidence in this table; no RCT-level evidence for finerenone (or any MRA) in IgAN has yet been identified.

**Real-world treatment patterns and outcomes.** A large retrospective regional electronic-health-record cohort of 978 adult biopsy-proven primary IgAN patients in China (identification period January 2015–June 2023) tracked treatment-pattern trends alongside clinical outcomes. Composite renal events (ESKD, 40% eGFR decline, creatinine doubling, or all-cause mortality) occurred in 6.65% (65/978) over a median 2.83-year follow-up, with a 5-year event-free probability of 90.5% (95% CI 88.2–92.9%); higher baseline and time-averaged proteinuria strata were associated with steeper eGFR-slope decline and higher composite-event hazard (e.g., time-averaged proteinuria ≥3 g/day HR 4.09, 95% CI 1.80–9.29, p=0.001, versus <0.5 g/day). Over the 2016–2023 study period, RAAS-inhibitor use rose to 91.49% of patients during follow-up, SGLT2-inhibitor uptake rose from negligible to 14.66% overall (17.14% by 2023), corticosteroid use declined from 36.62% (2016) to 16.49% (2023), and nonsteroidal immunosuppressant use rose to 31.89% by 2023 [Pan et al., 2026]. The study authors themselves note residual confounding by indication ("patients with more severe disease may have been more likely to receive intensive treatment"), missing data for several covariates, a relatively low absolute number of composite events limiting statistical power, and a regional single-network database scope that may miss out-of-network outcome events; the study was funded by Otsuka Pharmaceutical Development & Commercialization, a reviewer-identified potential industry-funding influence to weigh in appraisal. This real-world evidence complements, but does not substitute for, the placebo/active-comparator RCT evidence on individual drugs discussed above — it documents secular shifts in prescribing (rising SGLT2-inhibitor and nonsteroidal-immunosuppressant use, declining corticosteroid use) alongside proteinuria-stratified prognostic data, rather than testing any single intervention's efficacy.

**Overall trial-evidence synthesis.** Two tier-1 systematic reviews synthesize this expanding IgAN trial landscape: a PRISMA/Cochrane-methods systematic review of 183 studies (76 RCTs in narrative synthesis, search updated through December 2023) concluded that historical IgAN trial evidence was mixed/high risk of bias, while the newer NefIgArd and PROTECT trials demonstrate meaningful benefit, and explicitly called for further high-quality RCTs with ≥2-year follow-up — noting one co-author's affiliation with Travere Therapeutics (sparsentan's manufacturer) as an industry-adjacency flag for bias appraisal [Jeyabalan et al., 2025]. A formal international pediatric practice guideline (IPNA, GRADE-informed, treated in this table as an evidence-synthesis tier alongside tier-1 evidence) provides graded diagnostic and management recommendations for pediatric IgAN, but explicitly states that management recommendations in several areas are "supported by little high-quality evidence" — an acknowledged gap that should not be smoothed into a false sense of settled practice [Vivarelli et al., 2024, IgAN].

### IgA Vasculitis (IgAV)

#### Pathogenesis and mechanism

As background, IgAV shares overlapping mucosal-immune and IgA-glycosylation mechanisms with IgAN, though it is understood as a distinct systemic small-vessel vasculitis [Castañeda et al., 2024, background]. Directly on IgAV (not extrapolated from IgAN), a prospective case-control study of 86 pediatric IgAV patients and 70 controls (Croatia, 6-month follow-up) found that urinary HMGB1 was higher in patients who went on to develop nephritis than in those who did not, and correlated with albuminuria and hematuria, suggesting HMGB1 as a candidate biomarker for nephritis risk — though this is a single-country, pediatric-only cohort with generalizability to adults unestablished [Held et al., 2024]. A preclinical mouse study of Henoch-Schönlein purpura (IgAV) found that Rehmannia glutinosa extract and its active component catalpol reduced skin/kidney injury and abnormal IgA deposition via correction of a Treg/Th17 imbalance and suppression of PI3K-AKT-mTOR/NF-κB signaling, with accompanying gut-microbiome/metabolome changes; this is preclinical-only evidence (mouse, in vitro, network pharmacology) with no human efficacy or safety data [Lang et al., 2026].

Five further IgAV-specific mechanistic/biomarker studies substantially expand this sub-topic. A retrospective case-control immunofluorescence study of cutaneous biopsies (17 IgAV cases vs. 25 non-IgAV controls) found properdin (alternative pathway) positive in 82% of cases and C4d positive in 88% (with C1q positive in only 12%, suggesting lectin- rather than classical-pathway-driven C4d), and MASP1/3 deposition significantly higher than in controls; however, the study found **no significant association between complement-pathway activation and progression to IgAVN or response to therapy**, leading the authors to state explicitly that "it is unlikely that intervention in complement activation will lead to a better clinical course of the disease," tempering their own paper's "new target for therapy" framing [Damman et al., 2022]. This is a small (17 vs. 25), retrospective, cross-sectional cohort that cannot establish causality or predict clinical course, and this null clinical-correlation finding should be read as a caveat specifically on the therapeutic rationale for complement-pathway targeting in cutaneous/systemic IgAV manifestations, distinct from the renal (IgAVN)-focused complement/MEST-C evidence reported for IgAN above [Ștefan et al., 2023 — IgAN-specific, not extrapolated to IgAV].

A large case-control genetic-association study (381 Caucasian IgAV patients vs. 997 ethnically/sex-matched healthy controls, multi-center Spain) tested seven mucosal-immune-defence gene polymorphisms previously reported as IgAN susceptibility loci and found **no statistically significant differences** in genotype, allele, or haplotype frequencies between the overall IgAV cohort and controls, nor between the IgAVN subgroup and controls, nor across age-of-onset or GI/renal-manifestation strata. The authors conclude that these established IgAN risk loci do not represent genetic risk factors for IgAV susceptibility or severity, and suggest this reinforces IgAV and IgAN as potentially genetically distinct entities despite shared clinical/pathologic features [Batista-Liz et al., 2023]. This is a single-ethnicity (Caucasian) cohort limiting generalizability, and — as with any null finding — this sample size cannot rule out small-effect-size associations; this is nonetheless a notable, explicitly reported IgAN-vs-IgAV genetic-divergence data point, not a gap to be smoothed over.

A cross-sectional untargeted plasma proteomics study of 37 active pediatric IgAV patients (none with renal involvement at diagnosis or 1-year follow-up) versus 5 age-matched healthy children found 20 significantly differentially expressed proteins (of 348 quantifiable), with complement and coagulation cascades the most enriched pathway and the lectin-pathway marker MASP1 13.46-fold higher than in controls — converging with the complement-pathway findings above [Demir et al., 2023]. The study authors themselves state that "the major limitation of this study is the small number of the groups," explicitly framing it as a first-step, hypothesis-generating study; the **severe case:control imbalance (37 vs. 5)** substantially limits statistical power and increases false-positive/negative risk, and since no patients in this cohort had renal involvement, findings cannot be directly extrapolated to IgAVN specifically.

Separately, a mixed human-and-murine mechanistic study identified a pathway distinct from the complement-centric findings above: during acute IgAV flares, IgA-immune-complex binding to circulating neutrophils was elevated and primed a lowered NETosis threshold, an effect abolished by FcαRI blockade; **complement was not essential for this mechanism**. In a murine immune-complex vasculitis model, inhibiting NET formation and knocking out myeloperoxidase significantly reduced vessel damage [Mayer-Hain et al., 2022]. The authors frame IgA-immune-complex deposition as "mandatory but not sufficient" for IgAV, proposing neutrophil priming/NETosis as an additional necessary step; this complement-independent pathway should be read as a distinct, not necessarily contradictory, mechanism alongside the complement-centric IgAV findings in this table [Damman et al., 2022; Demir et al., 2023], rather than merged with them. Exact human sample size and specific p-values/fold-changes were not extractable from the available (paywalled) text for this study.

A fifth IgAV-specific case-control mechanistic/biomarker study tested a distinct infectious-trigger hypothesis: whether an immune response to nephritis-associated plasmin receptor (NAPlr) — a streptococcal antigen previously implicated in post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis — contributes to IgA-mediated vascular inflammation. In a cohort of 21 IgAV patients versus 18 healthy donors (with an n=11 skin-biopsy immunofluorescence/in-situ-zymography subset), NAPlr/plasmin-activity staining was positive in dermal lesions in 7/11 IgAV patients versus minimal staining in control skin; serum anti-NAPlr IgG and IgA were significantly elevated in IgAV versus healthy donors; anti-NAPlr IgG correlated positively with serum creatinine and negatively with CH50; and serum Gd-IgA1 was significantly elevated and correlated positively with anti-NAPlr IgA but negatively with C4. The authors conclude that these findings suggest an immune response to streptococcal NAPlr may contribute to IgA-mediated vascular inflammation, potentially via complement activation [Yoshida et al., 2026]. This interpretive conclusion is the study authors' own, not independently confirmed here: the study is a small (n=21 vs. 18; histology subset n=11), single-center (Japan), cross-sectional case-control design that cannot establish causal or temporal sequence — i.e., whether the anti-NAPlr antibody response precedes, coincides with, or follows renal involvement — and the correlations with creatinine and CH50 do not themselves establish that NAPlr is causally nephritogenic in IgAV rather than an epiphenomenon of broader infection-triggered immune activation. Full text was not obtainable (no PMC record found; the publisher landing page returned HTTP 403 on fetch), so extraction is abstract-level only, with the authors' own stated limitations, exact correlation coefficients/p-values, and funding/conflict-of-interest disclosures marked not extractable from the available text. This finding adds an infectious-trigger angle to the IgAV mechanistic evidence base, distinct from the complement-pathway [Damman et al., 2022], genetic-association [Batista-Liz et al., 2023], proteomic [Demir et al., 2023], and NETosis [Mayer-Hain et al., 2022] mechanisms already discussed above, and complements the background-context infectious-triggers review discussed elsewhere in this table [Filippatos et al., 2026, background].

A confocal-microscopy immunopathology study of 23 IgA vasculitis nephritis (IgAVN) kidney biopsies (17 pediatric, 6 adult, combined due to sample-size constraints) — part of the same underlying study reported separately for IgAN above [Novak et al., 2025 — IgAN] — found that C3-IgA colocalization was significantly greater than C3-IgG colocalization (p=0.0001), similarly to IgAN. In a direct cross-disease comparison, overall C3-IgA colocalization did not differ significantly between IgAN and IgAVN (0.712 vs. 0.658, p=0.142), but C3-IgG and IgA-IgG colocalization were significantly higher in IgAVN than IgAN (0.504 vs. 0.362, p<0.001; 0.585 vs. 0.468, p=0.001, respectively), and IgAVN biopsies showed more prominent peripheral capillary-wall deposits versus IgAN's predominantly mesangial pattern — a disease-discriminating finding reported here separately from, not merged with, the IgAN findings above, per this report's disease-separation constraint [Novak et al., 2025 — IgAVN]. Unlike IgAN, individual MEST-C/ISKDC components (E1, crescents) showed only non-significant trends toward higher C3-IgA colocalization in IgAVN, though the summed M+E+C score reached significance, as did (uniquely in IgAVN) the C3-IgG composite association; biopsies with diffuse endocapillary hypercellularity or ≥10% crescents showed higher colocalization than their counterparts. This is a small cohort (n=23, adult subset only n=6) combining pediatric and adult patients for statistical power, retrospective and cross-sectional in design, and — like the IgAN arm of this same study — carries the same unresolved corrigendum caveat described above: the Pearson-correlation and p-values reported here have not been checked against the corrigendum's correction [Novak et al., 2025 — IgAVN]. This mechanistic finding is consistent with, though not independently confirmed by, the separate clinical finding elsewhere in this table that endocapillary hypercellularity (E1) is the key MEST-C predictor of kidney outcome specifically in IgAVN [Barbour et al., 2024, discussed under Diagnostics below].

As further background, environmental and infectious triggers for pediatric IgAV — group A streptococcus and seasonal respiratory bacteria most consistently implicated, with lower-certainty, case-based evidence for other infectious agents — have been narratively reviewed elsewhere [Filippatos et al., 2026, background]. Directly within the included primary evidence, a single case report describes biopsy-confirmed IgA vasculitis (skin and renal involvement, with fibrotic crescent formation on follow-up renal biopsy despite clinical remission) developing during trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1, an anti-HER2 antibody-drug conjugate) therapy for HER2-positive breast cancer, treated successfully with prednisolone and cyclophosphamide [Watanabe et al., 2026]. The authors frame this as a novel drug-induced trigger for IgAV with pharmacovigilance relevance for oncology-nephrology teams. **This is a single case report, included via a lower-confidence, contestable tier-7 judgment call (a novel drug-trigger case rather than a therapeutic-efficacy study); it cannot establish T-DM1 as a confirmed, generalizable risk factor for IgAV and must not be generalized beyond this one patient.**

A newly identified mechanistic/preclinical study combining a pediatric clinical cohort, in vitro co-culture, and an in vivo rat model extends the IgAV mechanistic evidence base with a candidate cell-based therapeutic pathway. Children with acute IgAV (n=42) showed significant Th17/Treg imbalance (elevated RORγt/IL-17, decreased FOXP3/IL-10) versus 42 age-matched healthy controls; in vitro, IgAV patient serum increased IL-6/STAT3/p-STAT3 signaling and impaired endothelial wound healing in human umbilical vein endothelial cells and CD4+ T cells, effects reduced by human umbilical-cord mesenchymal stem cell (hUC-MSC) co-culture; and in an ovalbumin-induced rat HSP model, hUC-MSC treatment alleviated multi-organ pathology (skin, kidney, joint, intestine) and restored Th17/Treg balance via suppression of the IL-6/STAT3/RORγt axis, with a STAT3-agonist (colivelin TFA) co-treatment arm abolishing these benefits and thereby confirming STAT3-pathway specificity [Zhang et al., 2026, hUC-MSC/STAT3]. The authors themselves state that safety evaluation was "constrained by experimental duration and sample size," that long-term adverse effects and transplantation-related risks "require systematic assessment in extended studies," and that the pharmacological (rather than genetic) approach to STAT3 reactivation "does not exclude potential off-target effects." Critically, the human clinical cohort was cross-sectional/correlative only — no hUC-MSC therapy was tested in human IgAV patients; hUC-MSC treatment was validated only in the rat model and in vitro, so this is preclinical candidate-therapeutic evidence, not clinical efficacy evidence, despite the human cohort's role in establishing the Th17/Treg-imbalance rationale.

#### Diagnostics and clinical phenotypes

A key open question in IgAV diagnostics is whether the IgAN-derived Oxford MEST-C histologic classification applies directly to IgAV nephritis. A large, methodologically rigorous multicenter cohort (n=361, 23 centers, centralized 3-pathologist re-scoring) found that only endocapillary hypercellularity (E1) among the full MEST-C component set was independently associated with a high-risk eGFR-decline trajectory, while fibrous crescents (not part of the pediatric ISKDC classification) were also prognostically relevant — this **nuances rather than validates** the direct extrapolation of MEST-C from IgAN to IgAV, consistent with `research-question.md`'s instruction to treat such extrapolation as an open question rather than an assumption [Barbour et al., 2024]. A retrospective cohort with unsupervised hierarchical clustering (French IGAVAS database, n=260, independently validated on a Slovenian cohort, n=208) identified three distinct adult-onset IgAV phenotype clusters — young males with GI/joint involvement and low renal risk; middle-aged patients without GI involvement; and older patients with skin necrosis and high renal involvement/chronic renal failure — validated across two independent national cohorts [Maisons et al., 2026]. **This study carries an important caveat: a published corrigendum (Erratum) exists for this paper, and its existence and formal "Published Erratum" classification are now confirmed, but its substantive content has been confirmed inaccessible despite an exhaustive, multi-source verification attempt** (PubMed metadata, PMC, DOI resolution, direct publisher/PDF access, Crossref, Unpaywall, EuropePMC, web search, and the EFIM reprint page all failed to surface the corrected content); the numeric/cluster findings above have not been checked against the corrigendum, and whether/how they were affected remains genuinely unresolved, so any use of this study's specific figures should be treated with corresponding caution. A retrospective 2-center comparative cohort (n=165 total: 60 adult IgAV, 60 pediatric IgAV, 45 non-vasculitic IgAN, reported here as three separate rows per the source study's own subgroup structure) found that adult IgAV patients had higher overall long-term mortality than IgAN patients despite similar risk/time to dialysis — a phenotypic distinction directly supporting the requirement to report IgAV and IgAN outcomes separately rather than merging them; the pediatric IgAV subgroup had zero dialysis/transplant events during follow-up, severely limiting statistical power for any pediatric-specific comparison beyond that absence of events [Levanon et al., 2023, adult IgAV / pediatric IgAV / IgAN]. A multicenter retrospective study combined with a systematic literature review (1990–2025) characterized IgA vasculitis with necrotizing arteritis (IgAV-NA, n=30) as a distinct, severe phenotype — with higher multi-organ involvement and mortality than classical IgAV and some but not all features shared with polyarteritis nodosa — but the comparator cohorts (257 classical IgAV, 196 PAN patients) were historical/literature-derived rather than concurrently matched, and 23 of the 30 index cases were drawn from published literature with no formal risk-of-bias assessment, so ascertainment/selection bias is a material limitation on this study's comparative claims [Ribereau-Gayon et al., 2026]. A single case report adds an illustrative, low-weight data point to this same IgAV/necrotizing-arteritis overlap theme: a 70-year-old man presented with systemic inflammatory symptoms and purpura, with kidney biopsy showing IgA glomerulonephritis interpreted as an IgAV manifestation, concurrently with three arcuate arteries showing chronic sclerosing-phase necrotizing arteritis (a healed medium-vessel injury pattern compatible with, but not entirely specific for, polyarteritis-nodosa-pattern vasculitis). The authors present four competing clinicopathological interpretations (IgAV with atypical medium-vessel extension; a polyangiitis-overlap syndrome with two independent processes; PAN-pattern arterial disease with coincidental IgA nephropathy; or a shared infection-triggered inflammatory trigger) and explicitly do not resolve among them, concluding the evidence "establishes the coexistence... but does not establish their hierarchy, causal relationship, or precise nosological classification" [Fejes et al., 2026]. As a single case (n=1) with no skin biopsy/direct immunofluorescence ever performed to histologically confirm cutaneous IgAV, and with the kidney biopsy capturing only chronic (not active) arterial changes, this is the lowest-weight evidence on this phenotype in this table — it thematically adds to, but does not duplicate or independently confirm, the larger IgAV-NA case series above [Ribereau-Gayon et al., 2026].

Two further single-case reports add diagnostic-pitfall data points to IgAV clinical phenotyping, both illustrating the importance of not automatically attributing new or worsening findings during IgAV follow-up to the underlying disease itself. An 8-year-old girl with IgA vasculitis nephritis developed anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) nephritis that was initially misattributed to IgAV kidney involvement; the diagnosis was confirmed by markedly elevated anti-GBM antibodies and biopsy findings distinct from IgAVN, but despite plasma exchange and immunosuppressive therapy once recognized, kidney function progressed to kidney failure within several months. The authors' explicit teaching point is that worsening urinary findings or kidney function during IgAV follow-up should prompt consideration of an alternative or superimposed rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, with appropriate serological screening and timely biopsy, rather than automatic attribution to IgAV nephritis [Tanaka et al., 2026]. As a single case (n=1), this is included solely as an illustrative tier-7 diagnostic-pitfall example; it fits this carve-out more unambiguously (a competing-alternate-diagnosis pattern, directly analogous to rows discussed above for Hajji et al. and Fejes et al.) than the case described next.

A separate single case describes a 50-year-old man with biopsy-proven IgA nephropathy and clinically diagnosed (not biopsy-confirmed) adult-onset IgA vasculitis with purpura, polyarthralgia, GI involvement, and an apparent renal flare, who was escalated to rituximab after corticosteroid-refractory multisystem disease; after initial clinical improvement, he was readmitted with a fatal ST-elevation myocardial infarction and recurrent ventricular fibrillation arrests, with no new obstructive coronary lesion on repeat angiography and no coronary or myocardial histopathology obtained to confirm or exclude a vasculitic mechanism. The authors extensively discuss, but explicitly cannot resolve, whether these cardiac events reflect vasculitic/inflammatory coronary or microvascular involvement versus the patient's substantial pre-existing cardiovascular disease [Suleman et al., 2026]. **This case was included via a lower-confidence, contestable tier-7 judgment call: unlike the anti-GBM case above, its diagnostic-pitfall framing concerns causal attribution of a fatal complication to IgAV rather than a competing alternate primary diagnosis, and rituximab itself is not a novel intervention in this evidence base (already represented above). It must not be generalized into an epidemiological claim about cardiac involvement in IgAV from this single, multiply-confounded, fatal case.**

A newly identified large retrospective cohort (n=417 children with IgAV, 2015–2025, Turkey, EULAR/PRINTO/PRES-classified) examined predictors of recurrence (50 recurrent [12.0%] vs. 367 non-recurrent [88.0%]), finding that preceding infection (HR 2.33, 95% CI 1.20–4.51), microscopic hematuria (HR 3.00, 95% CI 1.62–5.57), widespread/bullous cutaneous involvement (HR 2.65, 95% CI 1.47–4.80), and joint involvement (HR 2.98, 95% CI 1.55–5.73) independently increased relapse risk, while lower monocyte counts were independently associated with increased relapse risk (HR 0.82 per unit, 95% CI 0.71–0.95) [Çelikel et al., 2026]. The study authors themselves note that "reported risk factors remain inconsistent across studies" in the prior literature, and that renal and gastrointestinal involvement — previously reported as recurrence predictors elsewhere — were NOT identified as significant predictors in this cohort, an inconsistency with prior literature that the authors flag but do not resolve and that this report likewise does not resolve. This is a retrospective, single-country, abstract-level-only extraction with no information available on blinding/adjudication of the recurrence definition.

Two further newly identified cohorts converge, from different angles, on a theme of pathological severity diverging from clinical indicators in pediatric IgAVN. A retrospective single-arm longitudinal case series (n=30 children with IgAVN undergoing repeat kidney biopsy at relapse, median inter-biopsy interval 39.95 months, China) found that despite no significant change in 24-hour urinary protein or urinary red blood cells from initial to repeat biopsy (both p>0.05) and even a slight eGFR *increase* (141.68 vs. 167.37 mL/min/1.73m², p=0.031), pathology showed significant deterioration: ISKDC grade worsened (p<0.001, with grade IIIb lesions rising from 26.67% to 50.00%), and chronicity indicators (global glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis) significantly increased (all p<0.05); clinical-indicator fluctuations did not sensitively reflect this pathological deterioration, and only eGFR fluctuation correlated with global sclerosis progression (p=0.019). Following biopsy-guided treatment adjustment in 80% of patients, both proteinuria and urinary RBC decreased significantly post-adjustment [Bie et al., 2026]. This is an uncontrolled, single-arm design — there is no comparator group that did not receive repeat biopsy/treatment adjustment, so the positive post-adjustment response cannot be cleanly attributed to the biopsy-guided change itself versus natural disease course or regression to the mean — and the selection process for which relapsing patients underwent repeat biopsy is not described in the available abstract.

Separately, a newly identified large single-center retrospective cohort (n=1,280 children with biopsy-proven IgAVN, China, 2013–2021) — the largest single IgAVN cohort identified in this evidence base to date — found crescents present in 70.5% of biopsies, with crescent-positive children more likely to be older and to have gross hematuria, gross GI bleeding, extrarenal manifestations, and severe renal involvement, higher proteinuria and D-dimer, and more frequent active/chronic pathological lesions; in a fully adjusted model, glomerulosclerosis, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, tubulointerstitial inflammation, endocapillary hypercellularity, and glomerular necrotizing lesions remained independently associated with crescent formation [Xu et al., 2026]. Specific adjusted odds ratios and confidence intervals for these associations were not extractable from the available abstract — full text was prioritized for retrieval given this cohort's size but remained paywalled despite repeated attempts, so this remains an abstract-level extraction. **This finding converges with, rather than conflicts with, the Bie et al. 2026 repeat-biopsy cohort immediately above: both studies, from overlapping Chinese pediatric IgAVN populations, support the theme that pathological severity (crescents, chronicity lesions) can diverge from or exceed what clinical indicators alone would suggest, underscoring the limits of clinical-only monitoring in IgAVN** — a consistent, not conflicting, cross-study pattern.

Finally, the HSPN/IgAV population (n=338 of 592) from the newly identified multicenter ultrasound habitat-radiomics diagnostic-accuracy study discussed under IgA Nephropathy Diagnostics above is reported here for completeness, per this report's disease-separation convention. **As stated above, this is not an independently-derived HSPN-specific result: the underlying study reports a single joint two-class (IgAN-vs-HSPN) differentiation model, with identical shared AUC/segmentation/feature-performance figures applying to both disease subgroups, and does not report HSPN-only accuracy metrics separately from the IgAN-only or pooled figures** [Gong et al., 2026] (see IgA Nephropathy Diagnostics above for the full model-performance figures). This diagnostic tool's good-but-imperfect discrimination (AUC 0.80–0.85) matters for IgAV specifically because IgAN and IgAVN carry different systemic-disease implications despite overlapping renal histology, even though the tool itself cannot yet reliably distinguish disease-specific subgroup accuracy.

#### Treatment

The strongest and most consistent treatment-evidence signal for IgAV kidney disease is a **convergent null finding across two independent, high-quality sources spanning different study designs and populations**: a Cochrane systematic review of 20 RCTs (1,963 participants, GRADE-assessed) found no significant difference in persistent kidney disease with short-course prednisone versus placebo/supportive care in children with minimal kidney involvement at presentation, no significant differences for antiplatelet therapy or for cyclophosphamide/MMF/tacrolimus comparisons in severe kidney disease, and no RCTs at all addressing prevention of recurrent IgAV kidney-disease episodes (overall low-to-moderate certainty; several comparisons underpowered, as few as 15–26 participants) [Hahn, Hodson & Craig, 2023]. Separately, the largest retrospective multicenter cohort of pediatric IgAVN identified in this review (n=1,148, 41 international centres, 25 countries, median follow-up 3.7 years) found that no specific second-line immunosuppressive regimen was superior to others, even in higher-risk subgroups [Rohner et al., 2024]. These two studies — a tier-1 randomized-evidence systematic review and the largest tier-4 observational cohort of its kind — independently reach the same conclusion using different designs and populations, which strengthens confidence in a genuine absence of demonstrated superiority among currently used immunosuppressive regimens for IgAV kidney disease, rather than being two isolated negative results. Consistent with this, the IPNA pediatric practice guideline's IgAV-nephritis-specific recommendations explicitly describe the underlying evidence base as sparse for IgAVN-specific management [Vivarelli et al., 2024, IgAV nephritis]. A newly identified adult-specific European multidisciplinary evidence-based practice guideline (14 recommendation statements plus overarching principles, systematic-review-informed expert consensus from a 31-author panel) fills this previously pediatric-only guideline gap for adult-onset IgAV, similarly stating that "the treatment of adult-onset IgAV is limited by the paucity of high-quality clinical trials and standardized therapeutic approaches," and identifying gastrointestinal complications as the main determinant of short-term outcomes and kidney involvement/CKD progression as the major determinant of long-term prognosis [Audemard-Verger et al., 2026]. **The specific content of this guideline's 14 individual recommendation statements (e.g., which drugs, thresholds, or treatment algorithms are recommended) is not extractable from the abstract-only text obtained this batch — full guideline text was not obtained — and should not be described further pending full-text review.**

A newly identified systematic review complicates this otherwise-consistent null-treatment-evidence picture. Pooling 41 patients with biopsy-proven IgA vasculitis nephritis from 28 published case reports/small case series (multi-database search through December 2023), it found a 92.7% overall remission rate (38/41) and 46.3% complete remission with rituximab, used predominantly as add-on therapy after glucocorticoids (37/41) rather than monotherapy; children achieved complete remission significantly more often than adults (76.9% vs. 32.1%, p=0.017); haematuria fell from 84.8% to 12.1% (p<0.001); and proteinuria, glucocorticoid use, and cutaneous/abdominal/joint manifestations all improved significantly — though the reduction in patients with eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73m² (42.3%→30.7%) did not reach statistical significance (p=0.425) [Xiong et al., 2024]. **This review is classified as tier-1 (systematic review) under the literal study-type ladder, but its underlying unit of evidence is entirely pooled, uncontrolled case-report/case-series-level data with no concurrent comparator arm** — the authors themselves state this is subject to substantial publication bias likely overestimating rituximab's efficacy, that follow-up timepoints could not be standardized across the pooled sources, and they explicitly call for prospective controlled studies; this tier-1 classification should not be read as evidentiary parity with the RCT-underpinned Cochrane review discussed above. The review's positive rituximab signal sits in **direct, unresolved tension** with the broader sparse/null high-quality IgAVN treatment-evidence base described in this subsection (the Cochrane review's low/moderate-certainty null findings and Rohner et al.'s null finding on second-line-regimen superiority) — this conflict is not reconciled by either source and is discussed further in Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty below. The review's own authors additionally note that a cited RCT — not itself an included study in this evidence table, and therefore not independently verified here — reported no significant renal-function/proteinuria benefit from rituximab in non-vasculitic IgAN, an IgAN-vs-IgAV discrepancy the authors themselves flag but do not resolve.

On the emerging-therapy side, telitacicept (dual BAFF/APRIL inhibitor) has the most substantive IgAVN-specific signal in this table, though at low evidentiary weight: the same adolescent case series discussed above reported an IgAVN arm of only n=4 (below the tier-5 case-series threshold, included only via the tier-7 novel-therapy carve-out), with no IgAVN-specific numeric results extractable separately from the pooled cohort figures — this should not be treated as independent confirmatory evidence [Wu et al., 2025, IgAVN arm]. A single-patient case report of telitacicept combined with glucocorticoids in adult-onset IgAVN reported a 78.7% proteinuria reduction and steroid-sparing effect at 6 months, but as an uncontrolled, unblinded, single-patient report this is the lowest-weight evidence in this table and must not be weighted as supporting evidence in its own right [Guo et al., 2026].

A further single-patient case report illustrates a management nuance in deciding when to treat IgAV with immunosuppression. A young adult man with biopsy-confirmed IgAV (perivascular IgA deposition on skin, ileal, and renal biopsies) had subnephrotic-range proteinuria alone, which by the authors' own stated usual threshold would not have warranted immunosuppression, but recurrent severe gastrointestinal symptoms (ileal ulceration on colonoscopy) together with biochemical evidence of hepatic involvement prompted a multidisciplinary decision to initiate a 6-week oral prednisone course; symptoms resolved, with complete normalisation of renal function and liver enzymes [Loudon et al., 2026]. The authors themselves state that "immunosuppressive treatment is typically reserved for significant renal involvement or refractory abdominal and joint pain," so treating refractory GI/joint symptoms is already standard practice and not itself novel; the authors also note that "therapeutic targets for gastrointestinal involvement remain poorly defined," and no liver biopsy was performed, so causal attribution of the liver-enzyme derangement specifically to IgAV (rather than an alternative etiology) is not conclusively established in the available text. **This is a single-patient case report (n=1) included via a lower-confidence, contestable tier-7 judgment call — on the same standard applied to the Suleman et al. 2026 and Watanabe et al. 2026 cases above — on the narrow basis that biochemical hepatic involvement served as an additional, less-established co-trigger for treatment alongside the already-standard GI indication; it must not be read as establishing hepatic involvement as a validated IgAV treatment indication from this one favorable outcome.**

A newly identified retrospective pharmacogenetics study (n=103 pediatric HSPN/IgAVN patients treated with tacrolimus plus corticosteroids, China) examined CYP3A5 genotype as a determinant of tacrolimus pharmacokinetics and clinical outcomes. CYP3A5 expressers (*1/*1 + *1/*3, n=52) had significantly lower tacrolimus trough concentrations and dose-corrected concentrations than nonexpressers (*3/*3, n=51) at all measured timepoints (p<0.01 to p<0.05), confirming a robust pharmacokinetic effect of genotype on drug exposure; however, there were no statistically significant differences in clinical efficacy (response rates 50.98–84.31% across genotype groups) or adverse-event incidence among genotype groups at any timepoint [Li et al., 2026, CYP3A5/tacrolimus]. The study authors themselves explicitly conclude that CYP3A5 genotype's "clinical relevance has not yet been demonstrated" despite the robust pharmacokinetic association, and their own post-hoc power analysis indicates the study was underpowered for several efficacy/safety comparisons (e.g., an estimated ≥69 participants per group would be needed to detect the expected adverse-event-incidence difference, well above the actual per-group sizes) — so this null clinical finding should be read as a real result requiring a larger study to confirm or refute, not as proof of an absent effect. This is a single-center, retrospective study with a particularly small CYP3A5*1/*1 subgroup (n=15); it adds a treatment-individualization/pharmacogenetics angle not previously represented in this evidence base's IgAVN treatment evidence, distinct from the biologic/immunosuppressive-regimen evidence discussed above.

**Extrapolation of IgAN-derived therapies to IgAV is discussed in the background literature but is not IgAV-specific evidence.** Two background/context narrative reviews explicitly map IgAN-derived therapies — TRF-budesonide, complement inhibitors (including avacopan, which as noted above has no IgAN or IgAV trial at all), and BAFF/APRIL inhibitors including telitacicept, blisibimod, and sparsentan — onto proposed IgAV-nephritis use [Castañeda et al., 2024, background; Williams et al., 2023, background]. Per `research-question.md`'s explicit constraint, this must be labeled indirect (IgAN-derived) evidence, not IgAV-specific evidence: none of these reviews report original IgAV trial data for these agents, and no included primary study in this evidence table tests TRF-budesonide, sparsentan, atrasentan, iptacopan, ravulizumab, cemdisiran, sibeprenlimab, or atacicept in an IgAV population.

## Quality of the Evidence

**IgAN** now has a comparatively mature tier-1/tier-2 evidence base: two tier-1 systematic reviews [Jeyabalan et al., 2025; Ștefan et al., 2023] and a near-tier-1 international practice guideline [Vivarelli et al., 2024, IgAN], plus four distinct placebo-controlled phase 3 trial programs (ALIGN/atrasentan, NefIgArd/TRF-budesonide, APPLAUSE-IgAN/iptacopan, and the active-comparator PROTECT/sparsentan program), and numerous phase 2/2b RCTs across BAFF/APRIL and complement drug classes. However, several of these "multiple" trial reports are the same underlying trial at different timepoints (PROTECT ×3, APPLAUSE-IgAN ×3, NefIgArd ×2) and should be read as one evidence stream maturing over time per drug, not as independent replications. All major phase 2/3 trials are industry-sponsored (Travere, Novartis, Calliditas, Visterra, Vera Therapeutics, Alexion/AstraZeneca, Alnylam, RemeGen, EMD Serono/Merck KGaA), which is a consistent, table-wide bias-appraisal note rather than an isolated flag. Mechanistic/pathogenesis evidence for IgAN (mucosal immunity, gut-kidney axis, novel mouse models) is exclusively tier-6 preclinical/mechanistic (human case-control or animal-model), useful for biological plausibility but not for efficacy/safety conclusions. This tier-6 base grew further in the Batch 3 incremental update (a B-cell-specific C1GALT1-knockout mouse model, a case-control immunophenotyping study with independent validation, and two additional preclinical target-discovery studies — Wu et al., 2026; Gentile et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025) but remains preclinical/mechanistic throughout. A subsequent Batch 4 incremental update added one further tier-6 preclinical study — a randomized, blinded rat-model study of the natural compound sarsasapogenin [Kumaş et al., 2026] — which, notably, features stronger internal validity (randomization, blinded outcome assessment) than most other tier-6 rows in this table, though it remains a single small (n=7/group), single-center, 12-week preclinical study with no human efficacy/safety data. Notably, one of these additions [Wu et al., 2026] directly nuances the Gd-IgA1-centered pathogenic model that underlies much of the IgAN therapy evidence above — a genuine mechanistic tension carried forward into Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty, not a settled refinement. A subsequent Batch 5 incremental update added a tier-4 two-center diagnostic-accuracy cohort for a candidate Poly-IgA ELISA biomarker (AUC 0.87, extraction limited to the abstract due to a paywalled full text) [Wang et al., 2026]; a large tier-4 real-world retrospective cohort (n=978) documenting IgAN treatment-pattern trends and proteinuria-stratified outcomes, industry-funded (Otsuka) [Pan et al., 2026]; and a tier-7 diagnostic-pitfall case series (n=6) of minimal change disease with mesangial IgA deposition, explicitly flagged as low evidentiary weight and not generalizable to typical proliferative IgAN [Hajji et al., 2026]. None of these three additions changes the overall tier-1/tier-2 trial-evidence picture for IgAN treatment described above; they add diagnostic, real-world, and diagnostic-pitfall context rather than new efficacy evidence. A subsequent Batch 6 incremental update added: a tier-6 confocal-microscopy complement-colocalization study correlating with MEST-C [Novak et al., 2025 — IgAN], which carries an unresolved corrigendum caveat (handled identically to the Maisons et al. 2026 precedent) on its reported Pearson-correlation and p-values; a tier-6 abstract-only TRIM21/mucosal-immunity mechanistic study [Chen et al., 2026 (TRIM21/AID class-switching)]; a tier-5 small (n=12), self-controlled, uncontrolled real-world telitacicept case series [Gu et al., 2026]; a tier-6 large (n=555), multicenter, outcome-validated digital-pathomics diagnostic study broadly comparable in rigor to, and extending, the Jaugey et al. 2023 MEST-C-automation precedent [Hölscher et al., 2026]; and a tier-5 real-world Nefecon case series (n=45) in an Asian-American population [Sun et al., 2026] that **carries a materially significant conflict-of-interest flag** — direct Calliditas Therapeutics (Nefecon manufacturer) funding with sponsor-employed co-authors, a higher industry-entanglement level than the sponsor-funded-but-independently-conducted pattern typical of the RCTs discussed above — that should be weighted accordingly rather than treated as independent real-world confirmation of NefIgArd. None of these five additions changes the overall tier-1/tier-2 trial-evidence picture for IgAN treatment; they add mechanistic, diagnostic, and real-world (COI-flagged) context. A subsequent Batch 7 incremental update added two further IgAN-specific records: a tier-4 single-center retrospective eGFR-slope cohort (n=180) identifying baseline albuminuria as the most consistent adverse predictor of eGFR decline, with a tentative, sensitivity-analysis-unstable Oxford T2-lesion association that the study's own authors characterize as "hypothesis-generating" rather than confirmed [Lin et al., 2026 (eGFR-slope cohort)]; and a further tier-6 exploratory human multi-omics study (renal-tissue proteomics, n=7 vs. 10; PBMC crotonylomics, n=6 vs. 25, unpaired cohorts) identifying candidate secretory-protein and lysine-crotonylation signatures, hub proteins, and upstream transcription factors, which the authors themselves state does not establish participant-level cross-tissue relationships, causal regulation, validated biomarkers, or therapeutic efficacy [Chen et al., 2026 (renal-tissue/PBMC multi-omics)]. Neither addition changes the overall tier-1/tier-2 trial-evidence picture for IgAN treatment; both are prognostic/mechanistic, hypothesis-generating contributions, consistent with the preclinical/mechanistic tier-6 pattern described above. A subsequent Batch 8 incremental update added two further IgAN-specific records, both abstract/metadata-level extractions due to paywalled full text: a tier-1 systematic review/meta-analysis (9 pooled retrospective cohorts, n=1,185) finding S/T/C — but not M/E or the total MEST-C score — consistently prognostic for allograft loss in transplant/recurrent IgAN, a distinct population from native-kidney IgAN and IgAV-nephritis that adds a third, divergent MEST-C-component pattern to this evidence base without resolving why the "robust" component subset differs across the three populations [Rath et al., 2026]; and a tier-4 multicenter retrospective cohort (n=788) finding a sex-specific association between low hemoglobin and ESKD progression (significant in women, not in men), a real reviewer-identified possibility being that the male subgroup was simply underpowered given only 66 total events [Yoo et al., 2026]. Neither addition changes the overall tier-1/tier-2 trial-evidence picture for IgAN treatment; the former adds transplant-population prognostic/diagnostic evidence and the latter adds biomarker/prognostic evidence, both consistent with this table's existing abstract-level-extraction pattern for paywalled recent publications. A subsequent Batch 9 incremental update added one further IgAN-specific record, this one with full text obtained (not abstract-only): a tier-1 retrospective cohort (n=63) combined with a systematic review/meta-analysis (6 pooled cohorts, n=311) finding that crescents on the native pre-transplant kidney biopsy do not predict IgAN recurrence after transplantation (pooled RR 1.53, 95% CI 0.40–5.90, p=.41) — a null result the authors themselves attribute possibly to low statistical power rather than a true absence of effect [Guzzo et al., 2026]. This addition is population/outcome-distinct from, and complementary to rather than in tension with, the Batch 8 Rath et al. 2026 finding on allograft-biopsy MEST-C lesions predicting allograft loss (see Findings above); it adds prognostic nuance to the transplant-IgAN sub-topic without changing the overall tier-1/tier-2 trial-evidence picture for IgAN treatment. A subsequent Batch 10 incremental update added two further tier-2 placebo-controlled phase 3 trials to this industry-sponsored pattern: ravulizumab's "I CAN" trial (sponsor Alexion/AstraZeneca Rare Disease Unit) and povetacicept's RAINIER trial (sponsor Vertex Pharmaceuticals) [Barratt et al., 2026, ravulizumab phase 3 interim; Barratt et al., 2026, povetacicept phase 3 interim]. **These two trials extend, rather than diversify, the table-wide industry-sponsorship pattern already noted above for every major IgAN phase 2/3 program (Travere, Novartis, Calliditas, Visterra, Vera Therapeutics, Alexion/AstraZeneca, Alnylam, RemeGen, EMD Serono/Merck KGaA): with ravulizumab and povetacicept added, every placebo- or active-comparator-controlled phase 2/3 RCT for an IgAN-targeted drug identified in this entire evidence base is industry-sponsored, with no independently (non-industry) funded phase 2/3 RCT in this evidence base to date.** Both are also, equally importantly, **prespecified INTERIM analyses reporting only surrogate/biomarker endpoints** (24-hour UPCR for both; Gd-IgA1 and hematuria resolution additionally for povetacicept) — not the eGFR-slope or kidney-failure hard-outcome data that, for iptacopan's APPLAUSE-IgAN program, only emerged in a later, separate final-analysis publication [Barratt et al., 2026, APPLAUSE-IgAN final] roughly two years after that program's own interim analysis [Perkovic et al., 2024]. This interim/surrogate-only staging — now shared by four of the complement- and BAFF/APRIL-pathway trial programs in this table (APPLAUSE-IgAN at its interim stage, ravulizumab phase 2, ravulizumab phase 3, and povetacicept phase 3) — should be weighted accordingly: a strong, statistically significant surrogate-endpoint signal at an industry-sponsored interim analysis is not equivalent evidentiary strength to a final hard-outcome result, and whether either ravulizumab phase 3 or povetacicept phase 3 will eventually replicate APPLAUSE-IgAN's hard-outcome benefit (and avoid its infection-safety signal) is not yet known. Both rows (75–76) are additionally abstract/metadata-level extractions only, since full text for both NEJM articles was paywalled with no PMC record obtainable. Liu et al. 2026's light-chain-deposition cohort (row 77, full text obtained) and Liu et al. 2026's exosomal C3a/C3aR mechanistic pilot (row 79, abstract-level only, no PMCID) add further prognostic/mechanistic, hypothesis-generating contributions consistent with this table's existing tier-4/tier-6 pattern; Tian et al. 2026's Nefecon+irbesartan case series (row 78, full text obtained) is explicitly self-characterized by its own authors as unable to isolate a combination-specific effect and is among the lowest-evidentiary-weight treatment rows in this table (see Findings above). None of these five Batch 10 additions changes the overall tier-1/tier-2 trial-evidence picture for IgAN treatment described above. A subsequent Batch 11 incremental update (2026-11-06, the largest weekly batch to date) added 15 further unique IgAN-specific source records (16 Included Studies rows, since one study is split across two disease-specific rows) spanning a materially more diverse evidentiary mix than prior batches. Most significantly, it added a new tier-1 PRISMA-2020-compliant, PROSPERO-registered systematic review/meta-analysis (8 cohorts, n=4,776) establishing persistent microscopic hematuria as an independent kidney-progression risk factor (pooled HR 2.64) [Berti et al., 2026] — a substantive strengthening of the tier-1 diagnostic/prognostic evidence base alongside the pre-existing MEST-C/complement systematic review [Ștefan et al., 2023]. It also extended the BAFF/APRIL drug-class evidence stream with belimumab, a real-world propensity-matched cohort showing a significant 12-month remission-rate benefit but a non-significant time-averaged-UPCR difference [Wu et al., 2026, belimumab] — real-world, lower-tier evidence that should not be weighted equivalently to the placebo-controlled interim RCTs already in this table for other BAFF/APRIL agents — and added a further preclinical complement-pathway candidate (a C5-targeted siRNA with a favorable non-human-primate head-to-head comparison against Nefecon) [Zhang et al., 2026, C5-siRNA], extending but not yet translating into human evidence for this mechanism class. Four further tier-4 retrospective cohorts added prognostic/diagnostic nuance: a pediatric RBC-cast cohort finding a counter-intuitive protective association (adjusted HR 0.51) that the evidence table explicitly flags as measuring a different hematuria construct from, not contradicting, the Berti et al. finding [Wu et al., 2026, RBC casts]; a Prognostic Nutritional Index cohort linking immunonutritional status to proteinuria and histologic severity [Kim et al., 2026]; and a finerenone+RASi-discontinuation cohort finding a proteinuria benefit from combination therapy accompanied by a non-significant but numerically worse eGFR-slope signal that the evidence table flags without resolving [Zeng et al., 2026]. Two further tier-6 mechanistic studies (NETs/cGAS-STING-driven glomerular endothelial injury [Li et al., 2026, NETs] and peroxidasin as a candidate interstitial-fibrosis biomarker [Silva et al., 2026]) extend the mechanistic evidence base without any accompanying human interventional data. A tier-7 diagnostic-pitfall case series (n=3) on ocular inflammation preceding IgAN diagnosis [Dhanshetty & Rapur, 2026] was judged a comparatively higher-confidence tier-7 fit than this evidence base's previously contested tier-7 inclusions, since it concerns diagnostic recognition of IgAN itself rather than managing an already-diagnosed complication. A tier-5 diagnostic-accuracy study (ultrasound habitat radiomics, shared jointly with the IgAV evidence base — see below) adds a non-invasive imaging tool for distinguishing IgAN from IgAVN with good-but-imperfect accuracy (AUC 0.80–0.85) [Gong et al., 2026]. None of these fifteen additions changes the overall tier-1/tier-2 trial-evidence picture for IgAN treatment described above, though the new Berti et al. 2026 systematic review materially strengthens the diagnostic/prognostic tier-1 evidence base, and the belimumab and C5-siRNA additions further diversify, without yet rivaling in rigor, the BAFF/APRIL- and complement-pathway drug-class evidence. A subsequent Batch 12 incremental update added five further IgAN-specific records. Most significantly, it added the first directly-verified rituximab RCT in this evidence base: a tier-2 multicenter trial (n=106, "RITA") finding no significant benefit of adding rituximab to RASi therapy on proteinuria, eGFR, or Gd-IgA1 at 12 months [Ouyang et al., 2026, RITA] — a trial whose abstract is unusually complete despite the full text remaining paywalled. It also added a tier-7 single case report of combined rituximab+daratumumab rescue therapy for refractory post-transplant crescentic IgAN [Zhang et al., 2026, rituximab+daratumumab], the lowest-evidentiary-weight addition this batch and not to be read as confirming either drug's efficacy; a tier-5 cross-sectional cohort (n=71) finding a fragile, inconsistently significant association between the systemic immune-inflammation index and acute histological lesions (significant only in a reduced sensitivity-analysis model, not the fully adjusted model) [Miyazaki et al., 2026]; and two further tier-6 preclinical/mechanistic studies — an M2-macrophage-derived extracellular-vesicle nanotherapeutic in a murine IgAN model [Zhang et al., 2026, M2 macrophage EV nanotherapeutic] and a human mechanistic study of CXCR3+ tonsillar T cells and Gd-IgA1 production supporting the tonsillar-focal-disease hypothesis [Wakisaka et al., 2026] — extending the mechanistic evidence base without new human interventional data for either. None of these five additions changes the overall tier-1/tier-2 trial-evidence picture for IgAN treatment described above, though the RITA trial materially changes this evidence base's rituximab-specific picture by converting a previously secondhand-only reference into directly-verified, if still null, RCT evidence (see Evidence Gaps and Conflicting Evidence below for how this interacts with the existing IgAV rituximab evidence).

**IgAV** evidence is comparatively less mature, though this update strengthens its mechanistic base. The single tier-1 source addressing IgAVN treatment via RCT-level evidence (the Cochrane systematic review) and the largest tier-4 cohort (Rohner et al.) converge on a null treatment finding, but this is now in explicit, unresolved tension with a newly added tier-1-classified systematic review [Xiong et al., 2024] reporting a 92.7% rituximab remission rate in IgAVN — a classification that is tier-1 by the literal ladder criterion only, since its underlying unit of evidence is 41 patients pooled from uncontrolled published case reports/series with no comparator arm and author-acknowledged substantial publication-bias risk; it should not be weighted as equivalent in strength to the Cochrane review. Diagnostic/phenotyping evidence for IgAV is dominated by tier-4 retrospective multicenter cohorts (Barbour, Maisons, Levanon, Ribereau-Gayon), several of which rely on historical/literature-derived rather than concurrently matched comparator cohorts — a limitation that should be carried forward whenever these studies' comparative claims are cited. IgAV-specific mechanistic/biomarker evidence has expanded substantially in this update: what was previously limited to one human case-control study [Held et al., 2024] and one preclinical mouse study [Lang et al., 2026] now also includes four further tier-6 studies — complement-pathway immunofluorescence [Damman et al., 2022], a genetic-association study [Batista-Liz et al., 2023], plasma proteomics [Demir et al., 2023], and a neutrophil/NETosis mechanism study [Mayer-Hain et al., 2022] — no longer the "comparatively thin" sub-topic flagged in the prior batch's saturation assessment. This remains, however, exclusively tier-5/6 case-control or cross-sectional mechanistic evidence, with several small and/or imbalanced sample sizes (Damman et al., 17 vs. 25; Demir et al., 37 vs. 5) that limit statistical power, and two studies reporting explicit null findings on their own primary hypotheses (Damman et al.: no association between complement activation and clinical course; Batista-Liz et al.: no genetic association at established IgAN risk loci) that should be represented as real findings, not gaps. Emerging IgAVN-specific drug evidence (telitacicept) rests entirely on an n=4 case-series subgroup and a single case report — both explicitly flagged in the evidence table as too small to weigh as independent confirmatory evidence. The Batch 5 update added one further tier-7 diagnostic-pitfall single case report on the IgAV/necrotizing-arteritis overlap phenotype [Fejes et al., 2026], explicitly flagged as low evidentiary weight and thematically additive to, not independent confirmation of, the larger tier-4 IgAV-NA case series [Ribereau-Gayon et al., 2026]. The Batch 6 update strengthens IgAV's near-tier-1 evidence base with a newly identified adult-specific, 31-author European multidisciplinary practice guideline [Audemard-Verger et al., 2026], analogous in evidentiary weight to the pre-existing pediatric-only IPNA guideline and filling a previously pediatric-only guideline gap for adult-onset disease — though its specific recommendation content remains unextracted pending full-text access (abstract-only this batch). The IgAV mechanistic base gained a further tier-6 confocal-microscopy complement-colocalization study in IgAVN kidney biopsies [Novak et al., 2025 — IgAVN], which also carries the same unresolved corrigendum caveat as its IgAN counterpart, and found a disease-discriminating pattern (higher C3-IgG/IgA-IgG colocalization and more peripheral capillary deposits in IgAVN than IgAN) worth representing distinctly per the disease-separation constraint. Diagnostic/phenotyping evidence gained one further tier-7 diagnostic-pitfall single case report with a high-confidence, unambiguous fit (anti-GBM disease misattributed to IgAVN) [Tanaka et al., 2026]. Two further tier-7 case reports were included this batch on an explicitly **LOWER-CONFIDENCE, contestable basis** flagged by `evidence-reviewer` to the orchestrator rather than a clear-cut fit like Tanaka et al.: a fatal adult IgAV case where the diagnostic-pitfall framing concerns causal attribution of a complication (a possible cardiac event) rather than a competing alternate diagnosis [Suleman et al., 2026], and a single case of IgAV triggered by the anti-HER2 drug trastuzumab emtansine, included under the novel-mechanism prong [Watanabe et al., 2026]. Both should be read with their one-line lower-confidence caveat attached wherever cited, and neither should be weighted with the same confidence as Tanaka et al.'s unambiguous diagnostic-pitfall fit. A subsequent Batch 7 incremental update added a third such tier-7 case report on the same **LOWER-CONFIDENCE, contestable basis**: a single adult IgAV case in which biochemical hepatic involvement served as an additional, less-established co-trigger for standard immunosuppressive treatment alongside already-standard refractory GI symptoms [Loudon et al., 2026]. As with Suleman et al. 2026 and Watanabe et al. 2026, this case must carry its lower-confidence caveat wherever cited and must not be weighted with the same confidence as Tanaka et al.'s unambiguous diagnostic-pitfall fit or generalized into a validated IgAV treatment indication. A subsequent Batch 9 incremental update added one further tier-6 IgAV-specific mechanistic/biomarker study testing an infectious (streptococcal NAPlr) trigger hypothesis via antibody/complement correlations in a small (n=21 vs. 18) case-control cohort, with an n=11 skin-biopsy histology subset [Yoshida et al., 2026]; full text was unobtainable, so extraction is abstract-level only (specific correlation coefficients, p-values, and funding/COI disclosures not extractable), consistent with this table's existing abstract-level-extraction pattern for paywalled recent publications. This addition strengthens, but does not resolve, the IgAV mechanistic sub-topic's existing pattern of small, single-center, cross-sectional case-control evidence [cf. Damman et al., 2022; Batista-Liz et al., 2023; Demir et al., 2023] and does not itself test or establish any therapeutic intervention. A subsequent Batch 11 incremental update added five further IgAV-specific source records, substantially deepening this evidence base's diagnostic/phenotyping and mechanistic coverage, though without adding any new controlled-trial-level treatment evidence. The largest single IgAVN cohort yet identified in this evidence base (n=1,280 children, crescent-formation correlates) [Xu et al., 2026] and a repeat-biopsy cohort (n=30) finding pathological deterioration despite stable clinical indicators [Bie et al., 2026] converge on a consistent theme of pathological severity diverging from clinical monitoring in pediatric IgAVN, though both remain tier-4/tier-5 retrospective, single-country, abstract-level extractions (the former despite prioritization for full-text retrieval, which was unsuccessful). None of these five additions changes the overall picture of sparse, controlled-trial-level IgAV treatment evidence described above; they add diagnostic, prognostic, and mechanistic depth without new RCT or cohort-level treatment efficacy data.

One inclusion in this table carries an explicit confidence flag rather than standard quality caveats: the Gandolfini et al. (2023) research letter was included as a tier-7 carve-out, and its classification as a data-bearing letter (versus pure commentary) was upgraded from LOW to MODERATE confidence following a targeted follow-up that found it pairs with "the authors reply" from NefIgArd's own senior investigators and is described by an independent, uncorroborated citing paper as reporting an approximately 10-patient case series with no benefit from TRF-budesonide over baseline transplant corticosteroids — but this remains short of HIGH confidence, this description is secondary-source-derived and NOT independently verified against Gandolfini et al.'s own (still-inaccessible) primary text, and it must not be weighted as a confirmed finding. A second inclusion, Maisons et al. (2026), carries a corrigendum caveat on all of its reported numbers: the corrigendum's existence and "Published Erratum" classification are confirmed, but its content has been confirmed inaccessible despite an exhaustive multi-source verification attempt, leaving the effect on the study's specific figures genuinely unresolved. A third inclusion, Novak et al. (2025) — reported separately above for IgAN and IgAVN — carries an analogous corrigendum caveat: a Published Erratum exists for this article, its existence and bibliographic linkage are confirmed via PubMed and Crossref, but its substantive content could not be accessed through either source, so the specific Pearson-correlation and p-values reported for both the IgAN and IgAVN cohorts have not been checked against the correction. A fourth inclusion, Berti et al. (2026), carries a bibliographic (not substantive) discrepancy rather than a content-access issue: the final author's surname is given as "Martitati" by PubMed and "Maritati" by Crossref; this is carried forward as "Martitati/Maritati F" without adjudication per the anti-fabrication rule against silently correcting a verified record's bibliographic details, and does not affect the study's reported findings or its tier-1 methodological strength.

## Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty

No head-to-head contradictory findings between two comparable primary studies were flagged in the evidence table (i.e., no case where one included study found a treatment effective and another comparable included study found the same treatment ineffective in the same population). The genuine tensions and unresolved points that should remain visible are:

- **ALIGN's internal near-miss.** The atrasentan ALIGN trial's key secondary eGFR endpoint narrowly missed its prespecified significance threshold (p=0.057) even as the total eGFR slope endpoint favored the drug — this is not a "positive trial" in the same unambiguous sense as APPLAUSE-IgAN or NefIgArd, and should not be described as such [Heerspink et al., 2026, ALIGN].
- **Surrogate versus hard-outcome discordance in PROTECT.** Sparsentan's proteinuria and eGFR-slope benefits versus irbesartan were statistically robust, but the composite kidney-failure (hard outcome) endpoint was not statistically significant at 2 years — a genuine gap between surrogate and hard-outcome evidence for this specific drug, distinct from iptacopan/APPLAUSE-IgAN, which did reach significance on a hard composite outcome [Rovin et al., 2023; Barratt et al., 2026].
- **MEST-C applicability to IgAV remains unresolved, not settled.** Barbour et al. (2024) nuances rather than validates direct extrapolation of the IgAN-derived MEST-C classification to IgAV nephritis; this should not be treated as either a validation or a refutation.
- **Narsoplimab's clinical trial outcome is unverified.** A citing commentary suggests the pivotal narsoplimab trial may have missed its primary endpoint, but no primary source was independently verified — this is recorded as an open uncertainty, not a finding either way.
- **Gd-IgA1 as a direct pathogenic driver versus a correlate of mucosal immune activation (IgAN).** A 2026 preclinical mouse study found that B-cell-specific genetic elevation of circulating Gd-IgA1 did not substantially enhance glomerular IgA deposition, and that tissue-of-origin (mucosal vs. serum/myeloma) rather than Gd-IgA1 level determined deposition potency in a passive-transfer model, leading the authors to characterize Gd-IgA1 as "a correlate of mucosal immune activation rather than a direct pathogenic driver" [Wu et al., 2026]. This sits in tension with the Gd-IgA1-centered multi-hit model described as background elsewhere in this report, with the mucosal-origin case-control findings [Zachova et al., 2022] and gut-dysbiosis findings [Zhu et al., 2024] in this evidence table, and with the biological rationale underlying several complement- and BAFF/APRIL-targeted IgAN therapies (rows testing iptacopan, ravulizumab, cemdisiran, sibeprenlimab, atacicept, telitacicept), which generally aim to reduce Gd-IgA1 or its downstream immune-complex effects. This is a single preclinical mouse study and does not overturn the broader mechanistic and therapeutic evidence base summarized above, but it is a genuine, unresolved tension that should not be smoothed over.
- **Rituximab in IgA vasculitis nephritis: a positive pooled-case-data signal versus a broader null-treatment-evidence pattern (IgAV).** A systematic review of pooled case-report/case-series data (n=41) found a 92.7% remission rate with rituximab in IgAVN [Xiong et al., 2024], while the Cochrane systematic review of RCTs [Hahn, Hodson & Craig, 2023] and the largest observational IgAVN cohort [Rohner et al., 2024] both found no immunosuppressive regimen demonstrably superior to others. These are not directly comparable study designs (uncontrolled pooled case-level data vs. RCT/cohort evidence), and the conflict is not resolved by either source — Xiong et al.'s own authors acknowledge the publication-bias risk inherent in pooling only published (presumably favorable) case reports, and 37 of the 41 pooled patients received rituximab as add-on rather than monotherapy, confounding attribution of remission to rituximab specifically. This should be read as an open question about whether rituximab's apparent benefit reflects a genuine treatment effect, publication bias, or concurrent glucocorticoid/immunosuppressant use — not as a resolved positive finding.
- **Three caveated inclusions bear directly on how much weight any citation of them should carry**: Gandolfini et al. (2023)'s classification as a data-bearing letter versus pure commentary was upgraded from LOW to MODERATE confidence based on a paired "authors reply" from NefIgArd's senior investigators and an independent citing paper's paraphrase (describing an approximately 10-patient case series with no benefit from TRF-budesonide beyond baseline transplant corticosteroids), but this remains short of HIGH confidence, is uncorroborated by any second citing source, and has not been checked against Gandolfini et al.'s own primary text, which remains inaccessible; Maisons et al. (2026) has a corrigendum whose existence and "Published Erratum" classification are confirmed but whose substantive content remains confirmed inaccessible despite an exhaustive multi-source verification attempt, leaving the underlying uncertainty about its specific cluster figures genuinely unresolved; and Novak et al. (2025, reported separately for IgAN and IgAVN) carries an analogous corrigendum, confirmed to exist via PubMed and Crossref but with content likewise confirmed inaccessible, leaving its reported Pearson-correlation and p-values similarly unresolved pending future access. Any specific number drawn from any of these three sources in future summaries of this report should be treated as provisional — for Gandolfini et al., this applies with particular force to the ~n=10/no-benefit description itself, since it is a third party's characterization rather than a direct extraction from the primary text.
- **Point-in-time RBC casts versus persistent longitudinal hematuria as prognostic markers in IgAN (not a resolved contradiction).** A newly identified pediatric cohort found red blood cell casts on biopsy independently *protective* against progression (adjusted HR 0.51) [Wu et al., 2026, RBC casts], while a newly identified tier-1 systematic review/meta-analysis found persistent longitudinal microscopic hematuria independently *harmful* for progression (pooled HR 2.64) [Berti et al., 2026]. The evidence table explicitly frames this as a directional tension between two different constructs — a one-time histologic marker of active glomerular bleeding versus a longitudinal urinary-sediment burden measured over time — rather than a head-to-head contradiction, since neither study tested the other's exposure definition. This should be read as a genuine, unresolved question about how different hematuria phenotypes relate to progression risk, not as one finding invalidating the other.
- **Belimumab's remission-rate benefit versus its non-significant proteinuria-magnitude benefit (IgAN).** A newly identified real-world propensity-matched cohort found belimumab associated with a significantly higher 12-month proteinuria-remission rate than standard therapy (93% vs. 80%, P=.025), but the corresponding continuous time-averaged UPCR difference was not statistically significant (P=.066) [Wu et al., 2026, belimumab]. These two outcome measures from the same comparison are discordant and should be presented together, not used to selectively support a positive-trial narrative; the study authors' own characterization of belimumab as potentially "superior" overstates what this discordant, observational, non-randomized comparison can support.

## Evidence Gaps

- **Avacopan:** No dedicated IgAN or IgAV clinical trial exists in the literature as of this search, confirmed via saturated searching (three consecutive reformulated queries, zero new candidates). This is a real gap in `research-question.md`'s own named scope, not an artifact of insufficient search effort.
- **Narsoplimab:** No full-text primary publication of pivotal trial results was found; only case reports and design-only conference abstracts exist in verifiable form.
- **Rituximab in IgAV and IgAN:** A tier-1-classified systematic review [Xiong et al., 2024] now provides pooled evidence (n=41) of a high rituximab remission rate in IgAVN, but this is uncontrolled, pooled case-report/case-series-level data, not RCT or cohort evidence (see Quality of the Evidence and Conflicting Evidence above). The most substantial *controlled or cohort-level* rituximab-in-IgAV dataset identified in this review's searches (a multicentre European cohort, n=61 IgAV + n=15 crescentic IgAN, reporting 85% remission at 6 months) remains conference-abstract-only with no matching full-text peer-reviewed publication identified — re-confirmed unchanged in the Batch 3 follow-up — and could not be verified or included per `research-question.md`'s exclusion criteria for conference-abstract-only findings. **Rituximab in IgAN specifically is now substantially, though not completely, addressed:** a directly-verified multicenter RCT (RITA, n=106) is now included in this evidence table and found no significant benefit of adding rituximab to RASi therapy on proteinuria, eGFR, or Gd-IgA1 at 12 months [Ouyang et al., 2026, RITA], closing the prior gap in which rituximab-in-IgAN content was available only secondhand via two included studies [Gentile et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2024] that each reference a different, external RCT not itself part of this evidence table (see Pathogenesis and Treatment above for how this distinction is preserved). What remains open: RITA was a single-country (Chinese), relatively short-follow-up (12-month primary endpoint, with only exploratory data to 24 months) trial, so longer-term and non-Asian-population rituximab-in-IgAN evidence is still absent, and RCT- or cohort-level rituximab-in-IgAV evidence (as opposed to the pooled, uncontrolled case-level data discussed above) remains entirely unaddressed.
- **PROTECT patient-reported outcomes:** A patient-reported-outcomes analysis of PROTECT could not be independently verified as a peer-reviewed publication (two independent PubMed searches returned zero results) and was excluded at the eligibility gate; patient-reported outcomes as an outcome domain are therefore not represented anywhere in this evidence base.
- **IgAV mechanism/pathogenesis:** This gap, previously represented by only one human study [Held et al., 2024] and one preclinical study [Lang et al., 2026], is now substantially addressed by four newly added studies spanning complement immunofluorescence, genetic association, plasma proteomics, and neutrophil/NETosis mechanism [Damman et al., 2022; Batista-Liz et al., 2023; Demir et al., 2023; Mayer-Hain et al., 2022] — see Findings and Quality of the Evidence above. What remains a gap: all IgAV mechanistic evidence in this table is tier-5/6 (case-control, cross-sectional, or preclinical), with no systematic review or RCT-level mechanistic synthesis specific to IgAV; and the one study that directly tested whether a mechanistic pathway (complement activation) correlates with clinical course found no association [Damman et al., 2022] — so the therapeutic relevance of these mechanistic findings to IgAV treatment decisions remains unestablished.
- **Long-term (>2-year) outcomes:** No included IgAN RCT reports outcomes beyond the ~2.5-year ALIGN/PROTECT/NefIgArd/APPLAUSE-IgAN timeframes; the tier-1 systematic review [Jeyabalan et al., 2025] itself calls for RCTs with longer follow-up.
- **Hard-outcome (eGFR-slope/kidney-failure) data for ravulizumab phase 3 and povetacicept phase 3:** Both newly identified NEJM trials report only prespecified interim analyses of surrogate/biomarker endpoints (24-hour UPCR; Gd-IgA1 and hematuria resolution for povetacicept) [Barratt et al., 2026, ravulizumab phase 3 interim; Barratt et al., 2026, povetacicept phase 3 interim]. No included study yet reports eGFR-slope or kidney-failure-composite outcomes for either drug — this is a direct gap in the current evidence base, not a finding, and should not be inferred from the surrogate-endpoint results already reported.
- **IgAN-specific stratified results for the Nefecon+irbesartan combination versus either agent alone:** Tian et al. (2026)'s case series reports only a single-arm, before-after combination-therapy result; no included study directly compares Nefecon+irbesartan against Nefecon monotherapy, irbesartan monotherapy, or an untreated/placebo control within the same trial, so whether the combination offers any benefit beyond Nefecon alone (already demonstrated in the placebo-controlled NefIgArd RCTs) remains unaddressed.
- **IgAN-specific stratified data within combined-cohort case series:** The telitacicept adolescent case series [Wu et al., 2025] reports only pooled IgAN+IgAVN figures; disease-specific effect sizes are not extractable from the available abstract for either arm.
- **Pediatric-specific quantitative IgAVN treatment-comparison data:** Beyond the null second-line-regimen finding in Rohner et al. (2024) and the IPNA guideline's acknowledgment of a sparse evidence base, no included study provides a positive, well-powered comparative treatment result specific to pediatric IgAVN.
- **Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism (finerenone) in IgAN remains entirely retrospective-cohort-level evidence.** Two included studies [Pei et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026] report proteinuria benefits from finerenone (alone or combined with RASi), but no RCT-level evidence for finerenone (or any MRA) in IgAN has been identified in this review to date; the numerically steeper, though non-significant, eGFR-slope decline in Zeng et al.'s combination-therapy arm is a specific unresolved signal warranting controlled follow-up.
- **Biopsy-guided management of IgAVN relapse lacks controlled outcome data.** Bie et al. (2026) found that repeat-biopsy-guided treatment adjustment was followed by improved proteinuria/hematuria in 80% of patients, but this uncontrolled, single-arm design cannot establish whether biopsy-guided adjustment itself (versus natural disease course) drove the improvement; no included study directly compares biopsy-guided versus clinically-guided management strategies in IgAVN relapse.

## Limitations of This Review

- **Non-exhaustive, bounded search.** Per `research-question.md`'s own saturation guidance for a literature of this size (thousands of PubMed hits per topic), searches were stopped at a representative, well-populated sample once high-quality systematic reviews/RCTs were captured, rather than pursued to full literal saturation for every sub-topic (avacopan was the only sub-topic taken to full 3-consecutive-query saturation). Additional relevant studies almost certainly still exist and were not captured. (An earlier version of this limitation flagged two specific candidate leads — C1GALT1-knockout mouse models and unconventional T-cell subsets — as identified but not yet verified/recorded per `sources/search-log.md` Run 16; both have since been resolved and are now included as [Wu et al., 2026] and [Gentile et al., 2024] respectively, per the Batch 3 incremental update described in Methodology. A further IgAV-mechanism sub-topic lead, rituximab-in-IgAV full-text availability [Trivioli et al. dataset], remains unresolved as of Batch 3 — see Evidence Gaps.)
- **English-language restriction.** Non-English studies were excluded per `research-question.md` unless an independently verifiable English translation existed; none were logged as excluded solely for language in this batch, but the restriction itself may have shaped which studies were discoverable.
- **Date range restriction (Jan 2022–present).** Foundational mechanistic literature predating 2022 is not represented in the Included Studies table (only used, where necessary, via background-tier sources).
- **Reliance on Consensus as a discovery tool.** Consensus (an AI-generated search summary) was used only as a discovery signal and never as a citable source in its own right; all bibliographic details were independently verified via PubMed before inclusion, per the project's anti-fabrication rules.
- **Abstract-level extraction for several studies.** A number of included records (e.g., Zhang et al. 2023 iptacopan phase 2; Wu et al. 2025 telitacicept case series; Lang et al. 2026 Rehmannia glutinosa; and, from the Batch 3 update, Wu et al. 2026 C1GALT1 mouse model, Mayer-Hain et al. 2022 NETosis mechanism, and Huang et al. 2025 SOCS1/TLR9/MyD88; and, from the Batch 5 update, Wang et al. 2026 Poly-IgA ELISA diagnostic assay; and, from the Batch 6 update, Audemard-Verger et al. 2026's adult IgAV guideline [specific recommendation content not extractable] and Chen et al. 2026's TRIM21 mechanism study; and, from the Batch 8 update, Rath et al. 2026's transplant-IgAN MEST-C systematic review and Yoo et al. 2026's sex-specific hemoglobin/ESKD cohort, both paywalled subscriber-only advance-access articles; and, from the Batch 9 update, Yoshida et al. 2026's anti-NAPlr/IgAV case-control study, paywalled via the publisher with no PMC record; and, from the Batch 10 update, both newly identified NEJM interim phase 3 RCTs (ravulizumab's "I CAN" and povetacicept's RAINIER), each paywalled with no PMC record, plus Liu et al. 2026's exosomal C3a/C3aR mechanistic pilot study, for which no PMCID was on record; and, from the Batch 11 update, Wu et al. 2026's RBC-cast cohort, Wu et al. 2026's belimumab cohort, Çelikel et al. 2026's recurrence-predictor cohort, Bie et al. 2026's repeat-biopsy case series, Xu et al. 2026's large crescent-formation cohort [despite prioritization for full-text retrieval], Kim et al. 2026's Prognostic Nutritional Index cohort, Zhang et al. 2026's C5-siRNA preclinical study, Gong et al. 2026's ultrasound habitat-radiomics study, Zeng et al. 2026's finerenone+RASi cohort, and Li et al. 2026's NETs/cGAS-STING mechanistic study; and, from the Batch 12 update, Ouyang et al. 2026's RITA trial [though its abstract is unusually complete, with little marked not-extractable], Miyazaki et al. 2026's systemic immune-inflammation index cohort, Zhang et al. 2026's M2-macrophage EV nanotherapeutic study, and Wakisaka et al. 2026's CXCR3+ tonsillar T-cell mechanistic study [exact sample size not stated]) had full text unavailable or paywalled, limiting extraction to abstract-level detail (exact sample sizes and statistical values explicitly marked not extractable in several of these rows); this is noted per-study in the evidence table and carried into this report wherever those studies are cited.
- **Four inclusions carry unresolved confidence or identity issues** (Gandolfini et al. 2023's letter-vs-commentary classification, upgraded from LOW to MODERATE confidence based on a paired "authors reply" from NefIgArd's senior investigators and an independent citing paper's uncorroborated paraphrase of an approximately 10-patient, no-benefit finding, but still not confirmed against the primary text, which remains inaccessible through eleven independent verification paths; Maisons et al. 2026's corrigendum, whose existence and "Published Erratum" classification are confirmed but whose content remains confirmed inaccessible despite an exhaustive multi-source verification attempt; Novak et al. 2025's corrigendum, confirmed to exist but likewise confirmed inaccessible in substance; and, newly this batch, Berti et al. 2026's final-author-surname discrepancy between PubMed ["Martitati"] and Crossref ["Maritati"], a bibliographic rather than substantive issue, carried forward without adjudication) that could, on resolution, change how those studies should be weighted, whether the ~n=10/no-benefit description attributed to Gandolfini et al. is accurate, or whether the Gandolfini record should remain in the Included Studies table at all. Separately, two Batch 6 tier-7 case-report inclusions (Suleman et al. 2026; Watanabe et al. 2026) were flagged by `evidence-reviewer` as lower-confidence, contestable fits to the tier-7 carve-out rather than unresolved verification issues — see Quality of the Evidence for the one-line caveat attached to each.
